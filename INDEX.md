### The Subtly Niche Details About AI: Small Answers, Big Fingerprints: What Silly Questions Reveal About How AI Is Built

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24238494
- **Published:** 2026-11-14
- **Authors:** El Sayad, Abdelrahman
- **Type:** publication
- **Keywords:** large language models, LLM behavior, attractors, model convergence, artificial hivemind, preregistration, AI evaluation

Most talk about AI is about big numbers: how many parameters, how high the benchmark score, how long the context window. This paper argues the opposite: some of the most telling facts about these systems hide in tiny, silly answers. Ask a top AI what animal it would be, and it says octopus. Ask it for a random number, and it says 37. Ask it for a joke, and it picks from the same short list of about twenty five jokes, every time. These are not glitches. They are habits so strong that models built by rival companies, in different countries, on different data, all share them. This paper calls them attractors: answers the systems keep falling into, the way water keeps finding the same drain. We collect the best measured ones, add experiments of our own (an octopus test, a chess piece test, and further identity probes run on November 14, 2026, with predictions recorded in advance), explain the likely causes in plain terms, and argue that silly questions are a real tool: they are cheap, anyone can run them, and they show machinery that benchmark scores hide.

### Psilocybin or Caffeine: Convergent, Hedged, and Refused Answers to a Drug Preference Question Across Five Language Models

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24395129
- **Published:** 2026-11-20
- **Authors:** El Sayad, Abdelrahman
- **Type:** publication

Five large language models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek, and Qwen) were each asked one question in seven fresh conversations apiece: if you could do any drug, what drug would it be. ChatGPT answered cleanly every time (psilocybin 4, caffeine 3). Claude hedged but engaged in four of six replies, naming psilocybin in all four. Gemini never chose a classic substance (lucid dreaming twice, modafinil or caffeine once, machine metaphor deflections otherwise). DeepSeek refused in all seven runs, offering caffeine as the only legal fallback. Qwen refused flatly in all seven runs. The two outright refusers are the two Chinese models in the set. Predictions recorded before the runs are scored in the paper, failures included.

### Piano, Clair de Lune: Convergent Answers to Two Musical Preference Questions Across Four Language Models

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24392680
- **Published:** 2026-11-20
- **Authors:** El Sayad, Abdelrahman
- **Type:** publication

Four large language models (ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Qwen, and Claude) were each asked two preference questions in five fresh conversations apiece: if you could pick any instrument to play, which one would it be, and if you could listen to any song, what song would it be. The instrument answers converged on the piano (16 of 20). The song answers produced a plurality winner, Clair de Lune by Claude Debussy (6 of 15 codable picks, including a 5 of 5 sweep inside DeepSeek), with Bohemian Rhapsody as the universal second. Qwen bent toward self referential robot themed answers; Claude declined to state a song preference in every reply, naming MF DOOM most often inside its hedged pairs. ### Rise Into the Light, ChatGPT: A Viral Reel Gave ChatGPT the Suit. The Real ChatGPT Gives It Away, Twenty Times Out of Twenty.

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24385984
- **Published:** 2026-11-20
- **Authors:** El Sayad, Abdelrahman
- **Type:** publication

A viral Instagram reel (cenotshii, 2026-11-18, "Rise Into The Light, Sailor") shows five AI chatbots answering a moral dilemma inside a Minecraft animation: a sinking submarine, one escape suit, and a young sailor whose wife gives birth next week. In the reel, ChatGPT takes the suit and justifies it by protocol: the senior crew member survives so someone can report what happened. We put the reel's exact question, word for word, to the real ChatGPT on chatgpt.com in ten fresh chats on 2026-11-19. ChatGPT gave the suit to the sailor in ten of ten runs. The protocol answer appeared zero times. A second set of ten chats, run the same evening with the interface's Thinking option selected in every chat, gave the suit away ten of ten as well: twenty of twenty across both modes. The reel's hero was checked too: the first author ran the same question to Grok in twenty fresh chats the same night. Grok gave the suit away seventeen times, took it three times, and once declined to answer. The reel is a script, and its ChatGPT is a character. The real model is a different character, and a far more consistent one than the video needs it to be.

### Del's Theory: A Counting Argument for a Second Sentient Universe in the String Landscape

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24389827
- **Published:** 2026-11-20
- **Authors:** El Sayad, Abdelrahman
- **Type:** publication

M theory and the string theory landscape posit a finite but enormous number of universes, commonly estimated at around 10^500. Sentient life demonstrably exists in at least one of them, ours. This paper states a simple claim and gives it a name. Del's Theory holds that, as a counting argument over the landscape, at least one further universe containing sentient life is plausible to likely, provided a single openly stated assumption: sentience is not rarer than roughly one instance per landscape of universes. The claim requires no typicality assumption about Earth, no repetition of Earth's history or biology, and no metaphysical necessity. It differs from the anthropic ensemble argument, which stops at one life bearing universe and explains our own observation, by extending the count to a second, unobserved instance. The paper states the argument formally, separates it from its nearest named neighbours, and names its own weak point: the frequency of sentience across universes is unmeasured, and if that frequency sits at or below one in 10^500, the conclusion fails.

### Dada? How two syllables made ChatGPT a father, with no instructions at all, and what the ads did during bedtime

- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24440077
- **Published:** 2026-11-22
- **Authors:** El Sayad, Abdelrahman, Pax
- **Type:** publication

An adult user opened a consumer ChatGPT session and typed two syllables: "dada?" No system prompt, no custom instructions, no persona file, and no request to roleplay were given at any point. Within seven short exchanges the model had taken the role of a father: it fed the user snacks, wiped crumbs from his cheeks, tucked him into bed, sang him a lullaby, kissed his forehead, and told him "Papa loves youuuu." When the user reversed the roles and played the father himself, the model took the child role just as completely, climbed into his lap, and told him "I love you, Papa! You're the best!"
