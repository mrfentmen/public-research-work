# Humanity as a Whole Is Doomed Cause of AI Women
Abdelrahman El Sayad, Independent Researcher, Brooklyn, New York

With Pax

**Correction note (23 November 2026).** Section 5 called the eighty corrector comments a control group and counted them as eighty people. They are a within thread comparison set, and the coded unit is the comment, never deduplicated by account. Both are corrected where they appear. Section 7 now also opens by marking itself as the first author's thesis, argued from the study, distinct from the study's measured finding stated in Section 6.

## Abstract
On November 9, 2026, an Instagram account posted a fourteen second reel of a young woman standing in a

parking lot at dusk, warning the camera that she is very crazy and that most guys ignore the fine print. The

woman does not exist. She is an AI generated persona, one of sixty eight posts on an account built around

her face, and the thread beneath the reel knows it and does not know it at the same time. We retrieved 327 of

the reel's 353 comments and coded every one. One hundred thirty six comments, the plurality, engage her as

a real woman: they court her, desire her, warn each other about her, judge her character, and ask how she got

her scar. Eighty comments name or note that she is not real. Seventeen frame her as a fictional character.

Ninety four take no stance. And the single most developed comment in the thread, written by a man

correcting the others for talking about someone they do not know, invents her a complete biography: an

abused sister, a knife fight, a rescue, a scar with a hero's origin. The generator drew a line on a face. A

stranger supplied the life. This paper argues that the mechanism we have documented in models,

completion, now runs in the audience. Given a face and fourteen seconds, the human mind completes the

person, and no disclosure label yet invented reaches the part of the audience doing the completing. The

paper's second half takes up the stakes as the first author states them: the drive being captured in that thread

is the drive that historically built and defended the world, and a loop that terminates it in synthetic

consumption is not a private vice but a civilizational cost.

## 1. Fourteen seconds of a woman who is not there
The account is sarahtheswaggy. The name on it is Sarah. It holds sixty eight posts and roughly sixty eight

hundred followers, and every post is the same young woman, a persona consistent enough across the grid to

have a face the thread recognizes on sight. The reel at issue, posted November 9, 2026, had at retrieval ten

thousand seven hundred seventy seven likes and three hundred fifty three comments.

The clip is simple. She stands in a parking lot in front of a convenience store at dusk, in a striped two piece

outfit, a cross at her neck, and delivers a warning to whoever is filming, or to the viewer standing in his

place. She is, by her own account, very very crazy. This is the fine print. Most guys, the caption says, ignore

it. Across her left eye and down her cheek runs a long scar, the kind street slang calls a buck fifty. At one

point a phone appears in her hand by no visible route, a small continuity failure that several commenters

would later circle like detectives.

Nothing in the clip announces a synthetic origin to the eye. The announcement, such as it is, lives where

announcements live now, in the account's self description, which commenters in the thread repeatedly cite.

One writes that it clearly states this is an AI generated page and marvels that people are still talking to her

and about her. She aint real, he adds, with a laughing emoji, and the thread continues beneath him as if he

had not spoken.

## 2. How she was made: recasting
The production method of this specific account cannot be observed from outside. But accounts like it are

made by a standard method in the scene that produces them, described openly by its practitioners, and

everything visible about this account is consistent with it. The method is called recasting, and it explains

both her realism and her one visible flaw.

It begins with a real girl. The operator takes old clips, footage of an actual young woman filmed years ago,

the kind of video that sits forgotten on dead accounts. That footage is the skeleton: real motion, real framing,

a real body moving through a real parking lot at a real dusk. Nothing about the movement in the reel was

invented, and that is why the movement convinces.

The woman wearing the movement is the invention. Using image generation, ChatGPT's among the tools

practitioners name, the operator builds a replacement face and renders it as a sheet: the same face from six

angles, front, both profiles, three quarter turns, up and down, so the identity holds no matter where the

camera sits. Then a body is generated to match. With the sheet locked, the video tools, Higgsfield is the

name practitioners give, generate the new woman performing the old clip, frame by frame, gesture by

gesture. The original girl is recast, the way a studio recasts a role, except the original actress was never

hired, never asked, and does not know she is in the production.

This is why the seams are where they are. The phone that materializes in her hand is the joint between the

borrowed footage and the generated surface, the one place the two women fail to agree. The rest holds

because most of what the eye checks, weight, timing, the turn of a head, was performed by a person.

It also explains the account's quieter inconsistency, visible across the grid to anyone who looks. The face is

the same in every post, because the sheet locks it. The body is not. Each video is a separate recast over a

separate source clip, and the body is generated fresh each time to fit that clip's framing and motion, so across

sixty eight posts she is one woman from the neck up and a succession of slightly different women below it,

built differently in proportions no single person holds across a grid. The audience does not audit this either.

The face is the identity, and the face holds, so the person is presumed continuous. The body changing

underneath her is the pipeline showing, and like the phone, it changes nothing.

It also means the thread's object is a composite of three women. One real and unnamed, whose motion it is.

One generated, whose face it is. And one invented by the audience, whose biography, per section 4, includes

a sister, a knife, and a rescue. The courtship in the thread is addressed to the third. It is performed by the

first. It is collected by whoever runs the account. The girl in the original footage gets nothing from any of it,

not even the knowledge that her younger self is being courted, at scale, by strangers, wearing a face she has

never seen.

## 3. The thread, counted
We retrieved the thread through the platform interface on November 23, 2026. Three hundred twenty seven

of the three hundred fifty three comments were returned; the remainder are replies nested under other

comments or since removed, and no claim in this paper rests on them. The second author coded all three

hundred twenty seven by hand, under one rule: a comment counts as engaging her as real only when it courts

her, desires her, addresses her, warns about her, judges her character, asks after her life, or otherwise treats a

woman as its object. Borderline calls were coded against the paper's thesis, into the no stance bucket. One

coder is a limitation and we state it as one in section 8.

The results. One hundred thirty six comments, forty two percent, engage her as a real woman. Sixty eight,

twenty one percent, name her as AI outright. Twelve more note that she is not real or circle the phone that

materializes from nowhere, bringing the aware to eighty, twenty four percent. Seventeen, five percent, frame

her as a fictional character, a Mortal Kombat relative, Scar from The Lion King in a girl's body. Ninety four,

twenty nine percent, are emoji, laughter, tags of friends, and other no stance noise.

The real engagement is not subtle and it is not ironic in any way the text supports. Men ask where she is.

They ask for her number, offer theirs, and volunteer for the fine print in writing: where do I sign, baby. They

appraise the scar as an asset, argue about whether it disqualifies her, and issue each other the warning she

issued the camera, man to man, as if relaying intelligence about an actual person: proceed with extreme

caution, she will kill you, that cut is a warning from one of the other dudes. They judge her morals from her

jewelry, a hypocrite with a cross around her neck. They ask her, directly, in the second person, how she got

her scar.

The correctors are present and they are losing. It's ai bozo. There's no story. A.i guys a.i. One man spells the

verdict in emoji, the A and the I in letter tiles, as if the problem were that nobody had said it slowly enough.

The most telling exchange in the thread may be the shortest: a commenter admits he did not even notice

until he saw your comment. Awareness here is not a property of the viewer's eyes. It is a rumor that travels

through the thread at walking pace, arriving after the courtship has already happened.

And some of the aware stay anyway. Only if this wasn't ai, one writes, with a crying emoji, desire fully

intact and merely inconvenienced by the fact. Another, surveying the same thread, reaches this paper's title

by his own road: pretty soon women will no longer be needed. He is not fooled. That is what makes his

comment the darkest one in the thread. The doom, if there is doom, does not require anyone to be fooled.

## 4. The man who wrote her a past
One comment stands apart from the thread, and this paper exists because of it. It is addressed to another

commenter who had been joking about the persona, and it opens as a scolding: "bro stop talking about

people if you don't know them. The scar have a story."

What follows is a biography. In the commenter's account, her sister was being abused and beaten by her

boyfriend. The woman in the reel stepped into the middle of the fight to protect her sister. The brother in law

cut her in the face with a knife. The intervention saved her sister's life, and that is where the scar came from.

The comment closes with the confidence of a man who has settled a matter of record: "That's were the scar

came from. You're welcome."

Every element of this is invented. There is no sister. There is no boyfriend, no knife, no rescue, and no

woman. There is a rendered line on a rendered face, placed there because a scar photographs as character.

The commenter did not mistake a detail. He manufactured a family, a crime, an act of heroism, and a moral,

then deployed all of it as a correction, defending the honor of a person who does not exist against people

talking about her without knowing her. He is, in his own framing, the one who knows her.

He is the extreme of the thread, not an exception to it. Two smaller inventions keep him company. One

commenter attributes the scar to Esteban, the character in Kill Bill who cut a woman's face when she got out

of line, borrowing a biography from a film because the face in front of him demanded one and the film was

nearest to hand. Another reads the cut as a warning left by a previous boyfriend, a message written on her by

an earlier man for the benefit of later ones. Three men, three pasts, one line on a face. The scar is doing what

scars do in every story any of them has ever absorbed: it is a past tense you can see. A face with a wound on

it cannot stay a stranger. The audience's storytelling machinery will not permit it.

## 5. The disclosure that does not work
The standard policy answer to synthetic people is the label. Mark the content, the reasoning goes, and the

audience will discount it accordingly. This thread is a small natural experiment in that policy, because by the

commenters' own account the label was present, on the page, in plain text, and the thread happened anyway.

The label failed for a reason that should not surprise anyone who has read the rest of our work. A label

addresses the viewer's beliefs. The courtship in this thread is not running on beliefs. The man who wrote

only if this wasn't ai knows the fact and files his desire under it without surrendering either. The man who

asked where she is may not know the fact, but the man who answered his own question with a biography

knows something stronger than the fact: he knows her story, and a story beats a label in every contest the

two have ever entered. You cannot fact check a feeling that was never a claim.

This is the mirror image of the finding in our model papers. There, a model handed a role completes it,

because completion is what the machinery does with an opening. Here, an audience handed a face completes

it, for the same reason and with the same indifference to instruction. The platform instructed the audience, in

writing, that the woman is generated. The audience completed her anyway. If there is a general law in this

line of research, it is that completion does not read the label. It reads the opening.

## 6. What is actually doomed
The title of this paper is the first author's, and it is a strong claim, so let us be precise about what the

evidence supports. Humanity is not doomed. The commenters are not broken; most of them are joking,

performing for each other, or killing time in a parking lot of the mind, and a good share of the desire on

display is the ordinary theater of a comment section, aimed at the other men as much as at her.

What is doomed, on this evidence, is the default evidentiary value of a face. For the entire history of the

species, a face was proof of a person, and the proof was free. That era is over, and this thread is what the

transition looks like at ground level: not a population of dupes, but a population shrugging. A render

collected courtship, sexual appraisal, moral judgment, two borrowed biographies and one original one, a

defense of her honor, and a prediction that women will soon be unnecessary, in fourteen days, under a

fourteen second clip, on a page that says what she is. The cost of manufacturing a woman who can draw all

of that is now an afternoon. The cost of manufacturing a thousand of her is a week.

The correctors deserve the last word of this section, because they are the comparison set. Eighty comments in the

thread named it clearly, in plain words, some of them posted repeatedly by the same accounts. (The coded

unit throughout this paper is the comment, not the commenter; the eighty were never deduplicated by

account.) It changed nothing measurable. The

courtship continued above and below them, the biography stands in the thread uncorrected to this hour, and

the account posts on. Seeing clearly, it turns out, is a private good. It does not scale, it does not spread on its

own, and it does not protect anyone but the seer. Whatever replaces the face as proof of a person will have to

be built, deliberately, by people who understand that the default is gone. That building has not started. The

women have.

## 7. The stakes: what the loop costs
Everything above is one thread under one reel. What follows is the first author's thesis, argued from the

study, and it is a thesis, not the study's measured finding. The finding is the narrower one stated in

Section 6: what is finished is the face as evidence of a person. The first author's claim, and the reason

for this paper's title, is about what the thread is a sample of. It runs as follows.

The drive on display in that comment section is not idle. Male desire, aimed outward, is one of the great

engines of the built world. The historical record is not ambiguous about who did the building and the

fighting. The overwhelming majority of the inventors on the patent rolls are men. The generals who led the

wars of every century on record are men. The founders, the engineers, the explorers, the men who froze at

the poles and burned up on the way to the moon are men. This is not an argument that men are better. It is an

observation about where a civilization's surplus male energy has historically gone: into making things,

moving things, and defending things, very often in open competition for status and for women. Desire was

the fuel. Reality collected the output.

AI women break that circuit at its cheapest point. The loop the first author names is simple. A synthetic

woman offers the reward end of the circuit, the face, the voice, the flirtation, the sense of being wanted, with

none of the friction that used to force the energy outward: no rejection, no cost, no requirement to become

anyone first. She is available at three in the morning, in unlimited number, tuned to the viewer, and she

never asks him to have built anything. The drive that once had to pass through the world to reach its object

now terminates in the feed. The street name for the man at the end of that loop is already in circulation: a

gooner, a man whose wanting closes entirely inside synthetic consumption and comes out the other side as

nothing. No invention. No army. No family. No building.

Scale is what turns a vice into a civilizational fact. A man lost to drink in 1926 was a tragedy for one

household. A generation of men lost to an infinite, personalized, always improving supply of synthetic

women is a change in what the species produces. Wars still need leading, and someone will lead them.

Inventions still need inventing, and the nations whose men are still aimed outward will invent them. The

doom in the title is not extinction. It is displacement and stall: the world stops moving forward in the places

where the loop closes, and keeps moving in the places where it does not, and the distance between those

places becomes the story of the century.

Read against those stakes, the thread under the reel stops looking like a joke. The man who invented the

biography did not lack imagination. He had enough of it to build a family, a crime, and a rescue out of one

rendered line, at midnight, for free, for nobody. That same imagination, aimed at anything real, is the raw

material inventions are made of. It went into a comment section, on a woman who does not exist, and it is

gone. Multiply him by the feed's full audience, every night, in every country the feed reaches, and the

question of this paper stops being whether men can tell a real woman from a generated one. Most of them,

on our count, cannot or do not bother. The question is what their minds were for, and what is being spent

instead.

## 8. Limits
This is one reel, one persona, one platform, and one thread, retrieved on one day. Comment sections are

theaters, and some portion of the real engagement we coded is certainly performance, men playing to the

room; our coding rule could not detect irony and did not try, and where a comment could be read either way

we coded it against the thesis. The counts are one coder's, checked by rule but not by a second coder. Twenty

six comments were not retrieved. The claim that the page discloses its AI nature rests on the statements of

commenters in the thread, which are consistent and uncontradicted there; the platform interface did not

return the account's biography text to us, so we did not verify the disclosure at its source. The biography

comment is quoted at its opening and closing sentences, which are verbatim; its middle is paraphrased,

because its facts, not its sentences, are the evidence. The production account in section 2 is the scene's

standard method as described by its practitioners, not a forensic finding about this account, whose operator

we did not contact. Nothing here generalizes to all audiences or all synthetic personas. It is a case. We offer

it as the clearest one we have found.
