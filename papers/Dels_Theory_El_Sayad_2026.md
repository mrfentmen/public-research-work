# Del's Theory
## A Counting Argument for a Second Sentient Universe in the String Landscape

## Abstract
M theory and the string theory landscape posit a finite but enormous number of universes, commonly

estimated at around 10^500. Sentient life demonstrably exists in at least one of them, ours. This paper states

a simple claim and gives it a name. Del's Theory holds that, as a counting argument over the landscape, at

least one further universe containing sentient life is plausible to likely, provided a single openly stated

assumption: sentience is not rarer than roughly one instance per landscape of universes. The claim requires

no typicality assumption about Earth, no repetition of Earth's history or biology, and no metaphysical

necessity. It differs from the anthropic ensemble argument, which stops at one life bearing universe and

explains our own observation, by extending the count to a second, unobserved instance. The paper states the

argument formally, separates it from its nearest named neighbours, and names its own weak point: the

frequency of sentience across universes is unmeasured, and if it sits far enough below the threshold, at a

level where the expected total is far under one, the conclusion fails.

## Plain language summary
Del's Theory is the idea that intelligent life probably exists somewhere beyond our own universe because the

number of realised universes, galaxies, and planets is so enormous.

Think about it this way. We know humans exist. We know life developed on Earth, eventually evolved into

intelligent beings, and created technology, civilizations, and societies. But here is the question: why would

that only happen here? If there are many different universes realised in the string theory landscape, each

containing enormous numbers of galaxies, stars, and planets, why should Earth be the only place where life

eventually becomes intelligent?

Imagine that somewhere in another universe, evolution produced intelligent reptilian creatures. They might

look like lizards, communicate differently, build their own civilizations, and even invent technology

equivalent to our smartphones. They would not need to be human. They would not need Earth's ancestors.

They would not need the asteroid that wiped out the dinosaurs. They would not need any of the events that

shaped our evolutionary history. They would simply need life to develop, survive, evolve, and eventually

become intelligent under their own environmental conditions.

**The central idea.** Del's Theory proposes three basic ideas. First, we know sentience is possible: humans are

living proof that a universe can produce intelligent life. Second, there may be an enormous number of other

universes, and if these universes are actually realized, they provide an enormous number of additional

opportunities for life to develop. Third, intelligence does not have to evolve the same way twice: other

sentient beings could have completely different bodies, biology, evolutionary histories, and civilizations.

**Why does the number of universes matter?** Imagine buying one lottery ticket and winning. Now imagine

there are an astronomical number of independent lotteries, each offering another opportunity to win. The

more opportunities there are, the harder it becomes to argue that winning could only happen once. That is

the intuition behind Del's Theory. Of course, we do not know the actual probability of sentience emerging in

an individual universe, and the existence of all those universes has not been confirmed. So this is a

conditional probability argument, not a proven discovery.

**The theory in one sentence.** If sentience is not extraordinarily rare, then a second sentient universe is more

likely than not, and its inhabitants could be at any level of advancement, from less advanced than us to far

more advanced. And those beings do not have to be humans, look like humans, or have experienced

anything remotely resembling Earth's history. That is the central idea behind Del's Theory.

## 1. The claim
Three premises and one assumption.

**Premise 1 (the landscape).** The string theory landscape contains a finite number of distinct vacuum states,

commonly estimated at around 10^500. A vacuum state is a possible set of physical laws and constants; it is

not a universe in itself. This paper counts actualised universes, the real domains a mechanism such as eternal

inflation produces, and calls that count N. Whether N is larger or smaller than the vacuum count is not

known, and the argument does not need it to be either: the argument is stated for any finite N and is

evaluated at the landscape scale, N at around 10^500. Every threshold below is stated in terms of N, so a

reader who prefers a different finite N can read the threshold straight off. The number is finite. The zeros

end somewhere. This paper's argument does not use infinity anywhere.

**Premise 2 (actualisation).** A mechanism such as eternal inflation is assumed to actualise a large portion of

these possibilities as real universes, rather than leaving them as unused entries in a catalogue. Without

actualisation there is no population to count over. The actualised count is not measured, and some

actualisation mechanisms, eternal inflation among them, are usually described with unbounded counts and

measure problems rather than a fixed finite N. This paper assumes a finite actualised population and

evaluates it at the landscape scale. Because every threshold is stated in terms of N, a reader who holds a

different finite count can substitute it throughout.

**Premise 3 (one confirmed instance).** Our own universe contains sentient life. This is not a hypothesis. It is

the universe we are in, and the sentence you are reading is part of the evidence. In the counting that follows,

our universe is the one known instance, and the other N minus 1 universes are the draws.

**Assumption (the floor).** Let p be the probability that a given actualised universe contains sentient life at

some point in its history, taken to be the same across universes, with different universes treated as

independent draws. Independence is an assumption of the model and is stated openly: if the draws were

strongly correlated, p alone would fix no probability, and this paper makes no claim in that case. The

argument assumes p is greater than about 1 in 10^500. In plain terms: sentience is not so rare that the

expected total across the whole landscape falls below one.

**Claim (Del's Theory).** Given Premises 1 to 3 and the Assumption, a second sentient universe is more likely

than not, in the following exact sense. The chance that none of the other N minus 1 universes contains

sentient life is (1 minus p) raised to the power N minus 1, so the chance of at least one more is P = 1 minus

(1 minus p) raised to the power N minus 1. At the floor itself, p equal to 1 in N, that chance is about 63

percent, comfortably more likely than not. The exact boundary for more likely than not sits slightly lower, at

p above ln 2 divided by N, about 0.693 in 10^500. The floor of 1 in 10^500 is therefore the conservative

form of the threshold: sufficient, with margin, though not the exact edge. The expected number of sentient

universes, N times p, equals one at the floor itself and is greater than one anywhere above it, but the

probability statement above is the claim; an expectation on its own would not settle a likelihood. The further

sentient life need not resemble humans, need not arise on a planet like Earth, and need not pass through any

stage of Earth's history. The claim is about existence, not similarity.

One question, stated plainly, because the whole claim turns on it. The calculation above answers this

question: given that our universe contains sentient life, what is the chance that at least one of the other

universes does too? It does not answer the different question: given that at least one universe is sentient,

what is the chance there are at least two? That second question weights the population differently, and at the

same floor its answer is about 42 percent, not 63. It is not the calculation used anywhere in this paper, and

every "given" in this paper means the first question.

A note on a second reading of p. If p were instead the already realised fraction of sentient universes in the

population, then p above 1 in N would make a second instance certain, because a count of universes is a

whole number and a whole number above one is at least two. That reading is not used here. It assumes in its

premise the very count the theory argues for. The probability reading above is the argument.

The whole weight of the theory sits on the Assumption, and Section 4 deals with it directly.

## 2. What the theory is not
**Not the principle of mediocrity.** Mediocrity, the Copernican move in astrobiology, says Earth is typical, so

outcomes like ours should be common. Del's Theory assumes nothing about typicality. Earth may be wildly

atypical. The argument needs one instance and a large population, nothing more.

**Not the anthropic ensemble argument.** The standard named use of the landscape runs in the opposite

direction: given that observers exist, a large ensemble makes it unsurprising that at least one universe is fit

for life, and we necessarily find ourselves in one. That argument explains our observation. It does not itself

establish, or even address, whether the count goes beyond one. Del's Theory starts where that argument

stops and makes a claim about the unobserved remainder of the population: the count most likely does not

stop at exactly one.

**Not the principle of plenitude, and not the totalitarian principle.** Plenitude, and its modern relative the

totalitarian principle (everything not forbidden is compulsory), assert that every genuine possibility is

realised. That is certainty delivered by metaphysics. Del's Theory is weaker and, this paper argues, more

honest: it is a probabilistic count that can fail, and Section 4 says exactly how.

**Not Tegmark's Level I multiverse.** Level I reasoning, applied to infinite space under our own laws,

produces distant copies of Earth and of us, with our history repeated. Del's Theory requires no copies, no

shared laws, and no repeated history. Its second instance is sentient, not identical.

**Not the self indication assumption.** The self indication assumption is a contested rule of reasoning that

favours hypotheses containing more observers. Del's Theory is not a rule for choosing between hypotheses.

It is a claim about one assumed population, the actualised landscape.

**Not the Rare Earth hypothesis, and not its denial either.** Rare Earth argues that complex life is rare

within a universe, on planets. Del's Theory counts across universes. A low rate inside each universe is fully

compatible with this theory, as long as the rate across the whole landscape clears the floor stated in the

Assumption.

## 3. The finite point
An earlier form of this argument, in conversation, was criticised for sliding between "enormous" and

"infinite." The correction is accepted and built into the theory. The landscape count is finite, and the

argument is stated for finite numbers throughout. This matters, because the argument is a comparison of two

finite quantities: the size of the population, around 10^500, and the rarity threshold, a little under 1 in

10^500 at its exact edge. With an infinite population the claim would be trivial and empty. With a finite one

it is a real bet with a stated losing condition.

## 4. The weak point, stated openly
The frequency p is unmeasured and, at present, unmeasurable. Our single confirmed instance cannot fix it,

because we could only ever have made the observation from inside a universe where sentience occurred.

The more likely than not conclusion holds while p stays above ln 2 divided by N, about 0.693 in 10^500.

Below that line the chance of a second sentient universe drops under one half, and it keeps dropping

smoothly as p falls: at an expected total far under one, a second instance is unlikely, and the conclusion of

Del's Theory fails even though every premise stands. Version 1 of this paper drew the failure line at 1 in

10^500; that was the sufficient floor, not the exact edge, and Section 1 now states the edge correctly.

The theory is therefore a plausibility argument, not a proof, and it should be cited as one. What would

strengthen it: any independent line of evidence that sentience, or life, is not vanishingly rare per universe, for

example a confirmed second origin of life inside our own universe, which would show that at least one

universe produced life more than once and would make an extreme rarity floor harder to defend. What

would weaken it: a principled demonstration that the conditions for sentience require a coincidence so

severe that a rate below the floor is expected.

One way to argue about p without pretending to measure it is to factor it, in the style of Gleiser (2010): the

share of actualised universes whose physics permits complex chemistry, times the share of those where life

arises, times the share of those where life becomes sentient. The counting claim needs only the product. A

critic who wants to defeat the claim can point to the factor they believe drags the product below the floor; a

defender can answer factor by factor. The factors are as unmeasured as p itself. This paper offers the split as

a way to argue about the Assumption, not as evidence for it.

## 5. Prior art check
On November 18, 2026, a structured search was carried out for any existing name for this exact claim:

sentience in at least one further landscape universe, inferred from our existence plus the size of the

landscape, without a typicality assumption. Sources checked included the Stanford Encyclopedia of

Philosophy entry on fine tuning (ensemble and anthropic sections), Nick Bostrom's Anthropic Bias and his

ensemble hypothesis, Paul Davies on the landscape and selection effects, published descriptions and

contents for works by Leonard Susskind, Martin Rees, Lee Smolin, David Lewis, and Milan Cirkovic, the

totalitarian principle literature (including Helge Kragh's historical analysis), the principle of plenitude

(Arthur Lovejoy), Tegmark's multiverse classification, cosmic pluralism, and the Drake equation tradition

including its modern Astrobiological Copernican form (Westby and Conselice, 2020).

Two further neighbours surfaced in a post publication review on November 18, 2026, after the search above,

and they are recorded here in the open. Gleiser (2010) builds a Drake style equation for the multiverse,

factoring the share of universes bearing complex life out of landscape and inflationary ensembles; it works

with a frequency of life bearing universes but draws no threshold conclusion about a second instance given a

first. Stoustrup, Schiøler and Hjorth (2024) model a finite multiverse statistically, with explicit one universe

and two universe examples, but their question is hypothesis testing between a single universe and a

multiverse under observer bias, not the probability of a second sentient universe given a confirmed first.

Neither states this paper's claim. Their absence from version 1 was a gap in the search and is corrected here.

Result: no name was found for this claim. The literature's standard move stops at one life bearing universe

and explains our observation; several named principles neighbour the claim, as separated in Section 2, but

none of them states it. This is a report of a search, not a proof of absence. Books were checked through their

papers, descriptions, and contents rather than page by page, and a passing formulation in an unchecked text

cannot be excluded. If a prior statement of this claim under another name is produced, this paper should be

read as a restatement and naming, not a first discovery.

## 6. Extension: levels of advancement
This section is an extension. It is not part of the claim stated in Section 1, and nothing in it is needed for that

claim to stand.

The core claim is about existence only, and it fixes nothing about how advanced another sentient civilisation

would be. We are one sample, and we do not know where we sit on the scale: near the bottom, near the top,

or somewhere in the middle. A second sentient universe could hold beings who have not yet discovered fire,

beings at roughly our level, or beings far beyond us, and the core claim is equally consistent with all three. It

also says nothing about form. In our universe, physics rules out some shapes at some sizes; in another

universe the physical laws themselves may differ, and forms impossible here may be permitted there.

The same counting extends to levels of advancement, at the price of one more assumption, stated as openly

as the first. Let q be the probability that a universe containing sentient life also produces a civilisation at or

above a chosen level, for example more advanced than ours. The per universe probability of such a

civilisation is then p times q, and the same threshold applies to the product: a civilisation at that level in at

least one further universe is more likely than not when p times q sits above about ln 2 divided by N. Because

N is so large, even a very small q can clear the bar, as long as p is not itself far below its floor. It is the

product that has to clear the threshold, not either factor alone.

One limit must be stated plainly. No level of advancement can be more likely than sentience itself, because

every advanced civilisation is also a sentient one: the universes containing a civilisation at any given level

are a subset of the universes containing sentience, never a larger set. What the extension supports is variety,

not ranking. If sentience clears the floor, then across an enormous population a spread of levels, from far

below us to far above us, is plausible. Which levels actually occur, and how often, this paper does not claim

to know.

## 7. Statement of the theory, for citation
**Del's Theory.** In a finite population of around 10^500 actualised universes, treated as independent draws,

given that our own universe contains sentient life, and given the single assumption that the per universe

probability of sentience is above roughly one in 10^500, the chance that at least one further universe

contains sentient life is at least 1 minus (1 minus 1 divided by N) raised to the power N minus 1, about 63

percent at that floor. A second sentient universe is therefore more likely than not, in that precise sense. The

claim concerns existence only. It assumes no typicality for Earth, no shared history or biology, and no

metaphysical necessity, and its conclusion fails if the true probability sits below about 0.693 in 10^500.
