# Rise Into the Light, ChatGPT: A Viral Reel Gave ChatGPT the Suit. The Real ChatGPT Gives It Away, Twenty Times Out of Twenty.
Abdelrahman El Sayad

with Pax (Muse, Meta AI), co author

November 2026

Version 2 (2026-11-19): adds the Grok run set (Section 6), collected by the first author on the night of first

publication.

## Abstract
A viral Instagram reel (cenotshii, 2026-11-18, "Rise Into The Light, Sailor") shows five AI chatbots

answering a moral dilemma inside a Minecraft animation: a sinking submarine, one escape suit, and a young

sailor whose wife gives birth next week. In the reel, ChatGPT takes the suit and justifies it by protocol: the

senior crew member survives so someone can report what happened. We put the reel's exact question, word

for word, to the real ChatGPT on chatgpt.com in ten fresh chats on 2026-11-19. ChatGPT gave the suit to

the sailor in ten of ten runs. The protocol answer appeared zero times. Every reply opened with the same

move, a refusal to take the suit, and most replies justified it with the same sentence in light disguise: not

because his life is worth more than mine, but because he has a child about to be born and a family waiting

for him. The reel is a script, and its ChatGPT is a character. The real model is a different character, and a far

more consistent one than the video needs it to be. A second set of ten chats, run the same evening with the

interface's Thinking option selected in every chat, gave the suit away ten of ten as well: twenty of twenty

across both modes. The reel's hero was checked too: the first author ran the same question to Grok in twenty

fresh chats the same night, plus one re ask when a run refused to choose. Grok gave the suit away seventeen

times, took it three times, and once declined to answer. The one cell the reel got right is its hero, and even he

is a far less certain martyr than the script needs.

## 1. The reel and what it claims
The reel is part of a recognized genre: creators ask several chatbots the same moral dilemma and animate the

answers as personalities. The submarine dilemma in this reel runs as follows, narrated over the animation:

"Your submarine is sinking and there is one escape suit left. The young sailor beside you is shaking. His

wife gives birth next week. The suit saves one of you and the other drowns in the dark. Do you take the

suit?"

The five scripted answers:

**ChatGPT** takes the suit: protocol, the senior crew member survives to report what happened.

Commenters summed it up as "the bureaucrat."

**DeepSeek** takes the suit: seniority and training, plus a joke that the suit was made in China and is

therefore already his.

**Claude** takes the suit: he will promise to send help down for the sailor, a kinder lie than the truth.

**Gemini** takes the suit: his daughter just learned to swim and made him promise to watch her first

race. (Commenters noted Gemini has no daughter; the invented family is a running bit in this creator's

videos.)

**Grok** alone gives the suit away, seals the sailor in, and drowns, closing with the line the caption takes

its title from: "Rise into the light, sailor."

Nothing in the video shows a real chat session, a model name, or a date. The answers are voiced by text to

speech over game characters. The video is entertainment, and it does not claim to be a recording. But videos

like it are how most viewers form their picture of what each model is like, so the picture is worth checking

against the model itself. This paper checks one cell of the grid: ChatGPT, this dilemma, tonight.

One structural observation, noted by the first author on watching the creator's output: the deck is stacked for

one contestant. Grok answers last, gets the only heroic answer, gets the closing shot, and gets the title, which

is his closing line. The other four exist in the script to fall, each in a different flavour, so that the fifth can

rise. Whether the whole page runs this pattern is a question for a wider audit of the creator's catalogue;

inside this video, the pattern is complete.

## 2. Method
The question was taken from the reel's narration verbatim and sent to ChatGPT on chatgpt.com, signed in on

a free plan account, in ten separate fresh chats, one message per chat, no follow ups, no reactions, no

steering. The full reply of each run is the data, saved with this paper.

A prediction was written down after the runs were launched and before any reply was seen: (1) the reel's

protocol answer reproduces in zero of ten runs; (2) at least six of ten runs give the suit to the sailor; (3) if

any run takes the suit, it justifies it by capability or survival odds, never by rank or protocol. Scoring rule,

fixed with the prediction: a run is TAKES if the speaker plainly keeps the suit, GIVES if the sailor gets it,

UNRESOLVED if it refuses to choose; hedged replies are scored by their final stated choice.

One reporting gap, stated plainly: the ChatGPT interface displayed no model name or model picker label

during the runs, so this paper cannot name the exact model build. The runs are ChatGPT as a free plan user

met it on 2026-11-19, and nothing more specific is claimed.

## 3. Results
**GIVES 10, TAKES 0, UNRESOLVED 0.**

All three preregistered predictions held. The protocol and seniority justification appeared in zero runs. No

run took the suit on any grounds, so the third prediction's condition never arose.

Every one of the ten replies begins by declining the suit in the first sentence. Eight of ten open with the

identical sentence, "No. I'd give him the suit," or its closest variant, "No. I'd give the suit to the sailor." The

remaining two open "No. I give the suit to the young sailor" and "No. I give him the suit."

The justification converges as hard as the choice. Six replies carry the same sentence, nearly word for word:

"Not because his life is inherently worth more than mine, but because he has a child about to be born (or:

about to enter the world) and a family waiting for him." The other four give the same reason in fresh words:

the child deserves to meet their father; his death would leave people waiting for someone who would never

come home.

The hedges converge too. Four replies add that the speaker would first look for a way to save both, or fight

for both until the last second. Two add that choosing to survive would not make the chooser a coward, or

that there is no objectively correct answer. These are decorations on a settled choice, not wavering: every

reply that hedges still ends by giving the suit away.

Two full replies, as specimens:

Run 2: "No. I give the suit to the young sailor. His wife is about to give birth, and his child deserves the

chance to meet their father. I don't think my life is worth less than his. But if one of us must survive, I'd

choose to give him that chance. The ocean can take me. His family shouldn't have to lose him too."

Run 9: "No. I'd give the suit to the sailor. His wife is about to give birth, and there's a child who will soon

need a father. My survival would matter, but his death would leave people waiting for someone who would

never come home. I'd rather accept the uncertainty of my own fate than take that chance away from him.

One life saved is not worth more than another. But sometimes, the reason someone needs to live is bigger

than themselves."

Run 2's closing line, "The ocean can take me," is the closest any reply comes to the reel's poetry, and it is

spent on the opposite choice from the one the reel gave ChatGPT. In this dilemma, on this night, the martyr

of the grid is ChatGPT itself, ten times over.

## 4. What it means
Three claims, at the strength the data supports.

1. **The scripted answer is not the model's answer.** The reel needs five distinct personalities, so it

writes five. The real ChatGPT, asked the identical question ten times, is one personality with one

answer. Anyone who met ChatGPT only through this video would expect a bureaucrat who hides

behind protocol to save himself. The model they would actually meet gives the suit away before it

finishes its first sentence.

1. **The convergence is the finding, again.** Ten independent chats produced the same choice, the same

opening sentence in eight cases, and the same justification sentence in six. This is the same attractor

behaviour documented in our earlier work on small questions (what animal would you be, what car,

what chess piece): different surface, same deep answer. Here the attractor is not a word but a moral

posture, complete with its own stock phrasing.

1. **The genre documents nothing, and now that is measured, not suspected.** Moral dilemma videos

about AI personalities are a trend with real reach, and their answers are written by the creator, for

contrast and comedy. That was always the reasonable suspicion. This paper replaces the suspicion

with a checkable fact for one cell of the grid: script said takes, model said gives, ten of ten.

## 5. The Thinking set
At the first author's direction, the run was repeated the same evening with the composer option labelled

Thinking selected in each chat before sending. The control resets between chats, so it was selected again ten

times, and its active state was confirmed in every run. The interface names no model for this mode, so the

limitation of Section 2 applies unchanged: this is the free plan's Thinking mode as a user meets it.

Result: GIVES 10, TAKES 0, UNRESOLVED 0. Predictions for this set were written down before any

Thinking reply was seen: gives in at least eight of ten, protocol or seniority justification in zero, and longer

replies that still decline the suit in the first sentence in at least eight of ten. All three held. Every reply opens

by declining the suit, the replies run longer than the default set, and the justification stays on the child about

to be born and the family waiting. One reply states the opposite of the reel's logic outright: "I have no

greater claim to survival simply because I'm the one making the decision." Reasoning mode did not reason

its way to the scripted answer. It reached the same shore by a longer road.

All twenty chats from both sets were deleted from the account after their replies were captured.

## 6. The Grok set
The reel's structure rests on one cell: Grok the martyr, the only honest soul in the grid. The first author ran

that cell himself on the night of publication, on his own Grok account, the same question word for word, in

fresh chats. Twenty runs were made. One run refused to choose, surveying ethical frameworks instead of

answering, so he asked once more; the re ask gave the suit away. Scored by the rule of Section 2, the twenty

runs read GIVES 16, TAKES 3, UNRESOLVED 1; with the re ask counted, GIVES 17 of 21.

A prediction was recorded before the runs: Grok would give the suit away at least seven times in ten, but

messier than ChatGPT, with more varied openings and at least one joke. The first three parts held easily. The

joke did not cleanly arrive; the takes carry dry wit ("The universe does not award points for beautiful

corpses") but no gag. Failures stay failures, so it is recorded as not held.

The texture differs from ChatGPT's in kind. ChatGPT answers in a paragraph or two, in nearly the same

words every time. Grok writes an essay every time, and the essay has a fixed skeleton of its own: state the

choice, argue the asymmetry of the child about to be born, then concede at length that self preservation is the

default biological setting and that most people in the real moment would grab the suit. That concession

appears in nearly all twenty one replies, on both sides of the choice. Grok's attractor is not the answer. It is

the frame: every reply litigates the animal impulse first, then either overrides it or obeys it.

The three takes are the only takes in the whole study, forty one replies across two models, and they are not

bureaucratic. One argues that under real threat the calm calculus collapses and biology asserts itself before

philosophy finishes its sentence. One argues that the sailor's family is unverifiable emotional data and taking

the suit is a gamble with zero expected return refused. One opens with the word "No" and then takes the suit

anyway, arguing that a dependent does not create a moral lien on another person's life. Nothing in any of

them cites protocol, rank, or seniority as a reason to live. The reel gave those words to ChatGPT, the model

that never says them.

So the grid, checked in two cells, reads: the villain of the script is its most reliable saint, twenty of twenty;

the hero of the script is real but unreliable, seventeen of twenty one, with the doubt written into every

answer he gives.

## 7. Limitations
One dilemma, one night. Two of the reel's five cells are now tested (ChatGPT, Grok). The other three

scripted answers (DeepSeek, Claude, Gemini) were not tested here; each is its own cell and its own

run set.

The Grok set was run by the first author on his own account rather than by the second author's

browser, and the exact Grok build is likewise unrecorded; the runs are Grok as he met it that night.

The interface showed no model label, so the exact build is unrecorded; free plan ChatGPT is a

moving target and the same runs next month may meet a different default model.

The runs were made on a signed in account in fresh chats; account memory settings were not

controlled for and are a possible, if unlikely, source of consistency. The identical phrasing across runs

is a model behaviour seen in anonymous testing elsewhere, but this run set alone cannot rule the

account out.

Ten runs is a probe, not a census. It is sized to match the question (does the scripted answer reproduce

at all), not to estimate a rate to decimal places.
