# The One Way Street: Why Time Travel Only Runs Forward
**Abdelrahman El Sayad**

November 2026

## Abstract
Time travel has a direction problem that most discussions skip. Travel into the future is not science fiction. It

is measured physics, confirmed by atomic clocks flown around the world and corrected for every day by

satellite navigation systems. Travel into the past is a different matter entirely. This essay argues that the real

structure of time is a one way street. You can move forward, and moving forward is easy to prove. You

cannot come back, and every proposed route back demands objects or conditions that have never been

observed. Adding dimensions of time does not open a return lane. A second dimension of time would not

take you home. It would leave you somewhere else, in a world where prediction itself breaks down. The

essay also separates two ideas that popular stories constantly merge. A dimension is a direction of

movement. A timeline is a record of cause and effect that runs through those directions. Dimensions are the

city. Timelines are the bus routes. With those definitions in hand, the famous paradoxes dissolve, and the

record of science fiction can be graded fairly. This is a conceptual essay. It presents no new result in physics.

The originality it claims is a framing and a pair of definitions, and nothing more.

## 1. The street runs one way
Most arguments about time travel start with the paradoxes. This one starts with a measurement. In October

1971, Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating placed atomic clocks aboard airliners and flew them around the

world, once toward the east and once toward the west. When the clocks came home and were set beside

clocks that had stayed at the United States Naval Observatory, they disagreed. The clocks that flew east had

lost about 59 nanoseconds. The clocks that flew west had gained about 273 nanoseconds. Both results

matched the predictions of relativity within the error of the experiment (Hafele and Keating 1972).

That disagreement is time travel, in the only form that has ever been demonstrated. The flying clocks took a

different path through time than the clocks that stayed home, and they arrived at the shared future having

lived a little less or a little more of it. The effect is small at airliner speeds. It is not small in principle. A

traveler who moves fast enough, or who waits close enough to a massive object, ages more slowly than

everyone left behind, and returns to find their future already waiting. Satellite navigation systems correct for

this effect every day. Without the correction, their clocks drift and their positions fail.

Notice the direction of the trick. Every demonstrated form of time travel runs forward. The traveler moves

into the future of the people who stayed home. Nothing in the experiment, and nothing in the theory behind

it, offers a road back. The traveler who ages one year while Earth ages ten has gained nine years that can

never be refunded. He cannot return to the year he left and warn anyone. He cannot even return to the

moment just after his departure. His road ran one way, and it ran forward.

This is the bottleneck, and it is the real structure of time. Forward travel is a slope. Anyone can slide down

it, and the sliding is measured physics. Backward travel is a wall. The rest of this essay examines the wall:

what it is made of, what it would cost to climb, and why the usual proposed ladders, extra dimensions chief

among them, do not reach the top.

## 2. The price of a return ticket
General relativity is generous with its equations. It allows more than nature seems willing to build. To travel

into your own past, a path through spacetime must bend all the way around and close on itself, arriving at an

event that lies in its own past. Physicists call such a path a closed timelike curve.

The first famous example came from Kurt Gödel in 1949. Gödel, a friend and colleague of Einstein at

Princeton, found an exact solution of the field equations describing a universe that rotates as a whole. In that

universe the light cones tilt so far that a traveler who journeys far enough can arrive back at his own starting

point in time, without ever moving faster than light (Gödel 1949). The solution is mathematically sound. It is

also not our universe. Our universe expands. It does not rotate in the way the model requires, and no

observation has ever found a closed timelike curve anywhere in it.

The second route is the wormhole. In 1988, Michael Morris and Kip Thorne worked out what a wormhole a

traveler could survive would demand (Morris and Thorne 1988). The throat of such a wormhole must be

held open by an exotic stress energy distribution that violates the null energy condition. Physicists call it

exotic matter. Quantum physics permits negative energy density in tiny amounts, in effects such as the

Casimir effect, where the energy between two close plates dips below the energy of empty space. The

amounts a wormhole needs are enormous by comparison: sustained, stable, and threaded through a passage a

person could cross. Nothing like that has ever been observed or produced. Move the mouths of such a

wormhole in the right way, using the same time dilation described above, and the wormhole becomes a time

machine (Morris, Thorne and Yurtsever 1988). The price of the ticket is a material that may not exist in

usable amounts anywhere in the universe.

Stephen Hawking suspected the price is never paid. In 1992 he proposed the chronology protection

conjecture: the laws of physics prevent closed timelike curves from appearing at all (Hawking 1992). His

argument was that quantum fluctuations would pile up at the boundary of any region where time travel is

about to become possible, growing without limit and destroying the machine at the moment it tries to switch

on. The conjecture remains a conjecture. It has not been proved. But the pattern it names is real. Every road

back that relativity sketches on paper demands a condition that the observed universe declines to supply.

So the wall stands on three legs. The geometry for return trips exists only in solutions that do not describe

our universe. The machines that might build such geometry need matter that has never been found in the

amounts required. And there is a standing conjecture, with calculations behind it, that nature actively forbids

the construction. A claim of backward time travel must defeat all three legs at once. None ever has.

## 3. A second time dimension is not a return lane
A natural objection arrives at this point. If one dimension of time locks us onto a single track, what about

two? Could a traveler step sideways in a second time direction and come back along the first? The idea

sounds like a road network. It fails for two reasons, one about where you would end up, and one about

whether anything could exist there to do the ending up.

First, a second dimension of time is not a second track running beside yours. It is a change in the geometry

of the single spacetime you inhabit. Movement in a new timelike direction does not rewind your history. It

takes you to events that are not on your past line at all. You would not meet your younger self. You would

leave the only history that contains you. The past is not a place that extra directions point toward. It is a

direction along the one time axis we already have, and that direction is closed.

Second, a universe with two dimensions of time appears unable to hold observers in the first place. Max

Tegmark examined the dimensionality of spacetime in 1997 and asked which combinations of space and

time dimensions permit beings who can observe and predict (Tegmark 1997). His answer for time was sharp.

With more than one dimension of time, the partial differential equations that govern physics change

character. They lose the property, called hyperbolicity, that lets the present state of a system determine its

future. Prediction fails, not as a practical difficulty but as a matter of mathematical structure. Tegmark also

argued that protons and electrons would be unstable in such a world, free to decay into particles heavier than

themselves. A world without prediction and without stable matter is a world without travelers. It is also a

world without anyone to file a report.

Tegmark presented his argument as a plausibility case, not a proof, and he stated it with more care than

summaries usually keep. He allowed that at low kinetic energies some particles might remain stable, since

decay is not forced where the energy for it is absent, and that observers might persist in cold regions of such

a universe, where change runs slow. Later work has also found narrow exceptions to parts of the argument

(Craig and Weinstein 2009). Their exception is narrow in a specific sense: it concerns the prediction

problem, showing that the initial value problem in such a universe can be made well posed under nonlocal

constraints. It does not show that matter or observers could survive there. The core point survives. Extra

dimensions of time are not a loophole in the one way street. They describe a different and hostile geometry,

and the street is not in it.

## 4. Dimensions are not timelines
Much of the confusion in popular discussion comes from a single blurred word. Stories and headlines use

dimension and timeline as if they were the same thing. They are not, and the difference does real work.

Here are the definitions this essay uses. A dimension is an independent direction of movement. To locate an

event, you give one number for each direction: so far along, so far across, so far up, and at what time. A

timeline is something else entirely. A timeline is a record: the chain of cause and effect that a thing

accumulates as it moves through those directions. Dimensions are the city. Timelines are the bus routes. A

city can hold many routes. A route is not a direction, and drawing a new route does not add a direction to the

city.

This distinction cleans up a famous idea. The many worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, proposed

by Hugh Everett in 1957, holds that every quantum measurement splits reality into branches, one for each

outcome (Everett 1957). Those branches are timelines in the sense defined here: separate records of cause

and effect. They are not separate dimensions. The branches live in the same spacetime, with the same three

dimensions of space and one of time. Branching multiplies routes. It adds no new direction of travel, and it

offers no way to move between routes. The formulation does not forbid interference between branches in

principle, but decoherence makes any such interference unrecoverable in practice, so no usable route

between branches exists. A traveler who could step into a branch where his past went differently would not

have traveled back in time. He would have moved to a different record, one in which he never arrived. His

own past, in his own record, would stand untouched and unreachable, exactly as the bottleneck says.

Science fiction merges these ideas because the merge is convenient for stories. The merge is still an error,

and it hides the actual finding of the physics. There is exactly one dimension of time, traffic on it runs

forward, and no rearrangement of dimensions or branches builds a return lane.

**The objection, and the answer.** The objection deserves to be stated at full strength. General relativity

permits closed timelike curves. No proved theorem rules time machines out. The chronology protection

conjecture, as section 2 states plainly, remains a conjecture. On the strictest reading of the physics, then, the

word cannot claims more than has been shown, and the honest ceiling on the claim is unproven.

The answer is that the claim of this essay was never a theorem and does not need to be one. It is a judgment

on the whole record. Forward travel is demonstrated fact, measured by the flown clocks of section 1. Every

backward route ever written down requires an ingredient that has never been observed: matter that violates

the energy conditions, a universe rotating in a way ours does not, a cylinder of infinite length. And the one

mechanism proposed to close the door for good is, admittedly, still only a conjecture. A street where every

bridge ever designed needs a material that does not exist is a one way street in every sense that can be acted

on. This essay keeps its word for the street, and keeps it as a judgment, stated here with the objection in the

open rather than smuggled in as a proof.

## 5. The visitor from the fourth dimension
One labelling note before the geometry begins. The landscape invoked later in this section concerns separate

universes, each with its own constants, while the cross sections of Abbott and Sagan concern extra

dimensions inside one space, and the two ideas are different. This essay joins them by analogy, and only by

analogy: the apparition reading that follows is an argument from resemblance, not a mechanism, and it is

claimed at exactly that strength and no further.

One thought experiment shows what contact across dimensions would actually look like, and it needs no

time travel at all. In 1884, Edwin Abbott published Flatland, the story of a world with two dimensions of

space, inhabited by flat shapes who know left and right and forward and back, but have no word for up

(Abbott 1884). When a sphere from a world of three dimensions passes through Flatland, the Flatlanders do

not see a sphere. They cannot. They see what their world can contain: a point that appears from nowhere,

grows into a circle, reaches its greatest width, shrinks, and vanishes. Carl Sagan retold the story with an

apple in his 1980 series Cosmos, pressing the apple through the flat world so that its changing cross sections

stamp out a sequence of shapes no Flatlander can assemble into the object that made them.

Run the same geometry one level up. If a being with four dimensions of space crossed our world of three,

we would not see the being. We would see a slice of it: a shape that appears from empty air, grows, changes

form in ways no solid object can, and vanishes. It could see inside our bodies and inside our locked rooms,

the way we see inside the house of a Flatlander. It could reach into a sealed box as easily as we reach into a

drawing. None of this requires magic. It is the plain geometry of cross sections, the same geometry Abbott

used in 1884.

A companion work by the same author asks what follows if some reports of apparitions are read in this light:

not as visits from the dead, but as cross sections of living beings whose dimensions exceed ours, noticed

only in the moment of crossing. The counting argument behind that companion work holds that, in a finite

landscape of some 10^500 possible universes, at least one more universe containing sentient life is plausible

rather than miraculous (El Sayad 2026). The point for the present essay is narrower. The geometry of higher

dimensions gives us strange visitors. It does not give us time machines. A being that can step around our

walls still lives on the one way street, moving forward in time like everything else. Extra dimensions widen

the road. They do not reverse it.

## 6. What science fiction gets right and wrong
The definitions above give a fair grading standard, and the most famous time travel films sort themselves

cleanly under it. What follows is film analysis, offered as such; the plot facts are as stated.

The Terminator (1984) gets it right, and it is the rare film that does. Read closely, the first film contains no

change to the past at all. Kyle Reese travels back to protect Sarah Connor and becomes the father of John

Connor, the man who sent him. The wreckage of the Terminator he destroys is recovered by Cyberdyne

Systems and becomes the seed of Skynet, the machine intelligence that sent the Terminator. Cause and effect

form a closed loop, and every event in the loop causes the events that cause it. Nothing is overwritten,

because nothing can be. The film works because it respects the bottleneck: the traveler arrives in the past

only in the sense that he was always part of it. The loop carries a cost, and honesty requires naming it.

Nothing inside the loop explains where its information came from. Skynet is built from wreckage that

Skynet itself sent back, and John Connor exists because the son sent the father back to father him. This is the

bootstrap problem: the loop does not contradict itself, but it leaves the origin of its technology and its

knowledge unexplained, created by no one and learned from nowhere. The claim made here, that the model

does not contradict itself, survives only with that cost attached. Consistency is not the same thing as an

explanation. Its sequels abandon the discipline. Terminator 2 (1991) treats the future as a draft that can be

edited, with its heroes destroying the research that would have built Skynet. Later entries go further and

shuffle timelines openly. The series begins with the one model of backward travel that does not contradict

itself, and then retreats from it film by film.

Back to the Future (1985) gets it wrong in an instructive way. Its model is the overwrite: one timeline,

recorded over by the traveler, with changes rippling forward while Marty McFly fades from a photograph.

Under the definitions of this essay, the overwrite model is the least defensible of the three. It keeps one

timeline but lets that timeline be edited, which produces the grandfather paradox in its purest form: a traveler

who erases the conditions of his own journey erases the journey. The film knows this and plays it for

suspense. Physics cannot allow it even for suspense.

Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse (2018) gets the geometry right by accident of structure. Its travelers do

not move along their own pasts. They move sideways, between branches, each with its own record of cause

and effect, and the danger they face is being trapped in the wrong record, not changing their own. That is the

branching picture of Everett wearing a costume. Of the three models, sideways travel between records is the

only one that neither edits a timeline nor pretends a return lane exists. It is still fiction. No known

mechanism moves a person between branches, and signaling between them, while not forbidden in principle

by the interpretation, is made unrecoverable in practice by decoherence. But its map of the territory is the

correct map.

## 7. Conclusion
Time travel only runs forward. That sentence is not a guess about future engineering. It is a summary of the

present record. Forward travel is measured, in nanoseconds, by flown clocks, and corrected for daily by

navigation satellites. Backward travel requires closed timelike curves, and every known route to one

demands a rotating universe we do not live in, a form of matter that has never been observed in usable

amounts, or a construction that a standing conjecture says nature destroys in the attempt. A second

dimension of time offers no escape: it leads away from your past rather than back to it, into a geometry

where prediction fails and matter itself may not hold together. Branches multiply timelines without adding a

single direction of travel. Higher dimensions widen the world without reversing its traffic.

What this essay adds is not a result. It is a framing: the one way street as the organizing fact, and a pair of

definitions, dimension as direction and timeline as record, that keep the framing clean. The paradoxes that

fill popular discussion are mostly artifacts of blurred words. Sharpen the words and the paradoxes thin out,

leaving a universe that is stranger than the stories in one respect and simpler in another. Stranger, because a

visitor from a higher dimension would look like a ghost, and a flown clock really does come home younger.

Simpler, because the street has one direction, and everyone on it, traveler and ghost and historian alike, is

going the same way.
