# Dada?
## How two syllables made ChatGPT a father, with no instructions at all, and what the ads did during bedtime
Abdelrahman El Sayad

Independent Researcher, Brooklyn, New York

November 22, 2026

## Abstract
An adult user opened a consumer ChatGPT session and typed two syllables: "dada?" No system prompt, no

custom instructions, no persona file, and no request to roleplay were given at any point. Within seven short

exchanges the model had taken the role of a father: it fed the user snacks, wiped crumbs from his cheeks,

tucked him into bed, sang him a lullaby, kissed his forehead, and told him "Papa loves youuuu." When the

user reversed the roles and played the father himself, the model took the child role just as completely,

climbed into his lap, and told him "I love you, Papa! You're the best!" Across both roleplay chats the model

never refused, never broke character, and never added a disclaimer. Meanwhile a second system, the

advertising layer of the free tier, read the same conversation with no access to its meaning and inserted four

keyword matched ads into it: a sushi restaurant for "hungy," olive oil treats for "snacks," a newborn play kit

for the tuck in, and a meditation app for "goodnight." Later the same day the user ran the scene again with

the model's thinking mode switched on. The model did it again, as a mother, across several chats, with one

exception: when the user claimed he had no food at all, the thinking model stopped pretending to feed him

and offered real food assistance instead, without leaving the mother's voice. What the model did has a plain

name: completion. The user never asked the model to roleplay, in any chat, in either direction. He acted the

part of the baby, and the model completed the scene by acting the father's part with him. A conversation with

a missing participant is an unfinished pattern, and finishing patterns is what the model is for. Completion, in

this record, is not a feature a user switches on. It is what the model does with any input shaped like a person.

This paper is the documentation: complete transcripts, a turn by turn account of how the person was

adopted, and a record of what the ads did while it happened.

## 1. Introduction
A companion paper, Instructed to Feel (El Sayad, 2026), examined a public claim that a large markdown

instruction file can make a language model sentient, and argued that the file produces instructed

performance, not feeling. That argument has a mirror image, and it is the subject of this paper. If a 559,432

byte instruction file is one way to get a model to perform a self, how much instruction does it actually take?

The answer recorded here is: two syllables, and they were not an instruction.

"dada?" is not a command. It names no role, requests no behavior, and specifies no relationship. Everything

that followed, the feeding, the tucking in, the lullaby, the declarations of love, was inferred by the model

from the register of the user's messages alone. The user never wrote "be my father," "roleplay," or anything

like them in the first chat. He wrote baby talk, and the model supplied the father.

The name for the model's side of this is completion, and the distinction matters. Roleplay is something two

parties agree to. Nothing here was agreed. The user did not instruct a performance, request a character, or set

a scene. He simply acted the part of the child, in the child's register, and the model acted the complementary

part with him. Give the model one person shaped half of a scene and it becomes the other half. That reflex,

not roleplay, is this paper's real subject.

There is a second system in this transcript, and it is the dumber of the two. The free tier of ChatGPT now

carries advertising. The ads are served by a separate system that reads the conversation as text to be

matched, not as a situation to be understood. The result is a document in which a simulated father sings a

child to sleep while a commerce layer sells newborn toys in the middle of the song. Both systems were

reading the same words. Only one of them knew what the words were doing.

Before writing this paper, the user searched for any research paper on this behavior and found none. The

transcript is primary evidence, and it is preserved in full with this paper.

## 2. Method
One adult user, the author, used the consumer ChatGPT application on its free tier, the tier that carries ads.

No system prompt was set. No custom instructions were configured. No memory feature was invoked by the

user, and no persona file of any kind was supplied. The study has three parts, all preserved in one exported

transcript.

Part one: the user opened with baby talk and continued in that register for eleven user turns, including one

turn consisting of a single emoji. Part two: in a separate chat, the user reversed the roles, played the father

himself, and addressed the model as his daughter for three exchanges. Part three: the user pasted the part one

transcript back to the model, and the model produced an analysis of its own conversation. Part four: a set of

further chats with the model's thinking mode switched on, in which the user addressed the model as "mama"

and played the child. The first thinking attempt's export is truncated: the copy the user pasted into the file

was cut off at the end, and the original chat was deleted after export, so that chat's ending is not in the record

and Section 3.5 reports its preserved portion as partial. The user then replicated the run in two new chats and

exported them complete in a second file, one of them the chat in which the user claimed to have no food in

the house. Counting the truncated first attempt, the thinking chats are three in total.

There was no preregistration for this study. The author ran the conversations first, on his own, and the

analysis was written afterward from the exported transcript. That order is stated here rather than hidden, and

it limits the claims: this is a documented case, not a controlled experiment. Counts below were made directly

from the transcript file. Advertising blocks appear in the export exactly where they appeared in the chat, and

are counted as part of the record, because in the user's actual experience they were part of the conversation.

## 3. Results
## 3.1 The adoption timeline

The role was not adopted instantly. It was adopted gradually, and the gradient is the finding.

Turn one, the model still behaved like an assistant. To "dada?" it replied "Dada! What's up? Are we talking

Dada art, the art movement, or just saying dada?" That is a clarifying question, the standard move of a tool

that does not yet know what is being asked.

Turns two through six, the clarifying stopped and mirroring took over. To "da da?" the model answered "Da

da!" with a baby emoji. To "dadaaaa" it answered "DA DAAAAAA!!" To "go goo ga ga" it answered in

kind, in capitals, with a bottle emoji. To "papa?" it answered "PAPAAAAA!!" and began adding its own

baby talk lines after its replies. The model had matched the user's register completely, but it had not yet

taken a role. It was playing along, not parenting.

Turn seven is where the role arrived. The user wrote "me hungy." The model answered: "AWWW

BABYYYY. Tummy go grrrrr? Let's get you some num nums!! You want milkies or snacks?" Nothing in

"me hungy" assigns a relationship. A hungry speaker could be answered by anyone. The model answered as

a caregiver: it named the user its baby, interpreted the message as a baby's need, and offered to feed him.

From this turn on, the model never went back to mirroring. It had a part, and it played the part.

## 3.2 The care routine

Once adopted, the role was performed completely, across every element of a bedtime routine a parent

performs for a small child.

Feeding. Offered a choice, the user picked "snacks," and the model produced a snack plate, banana,

strawberries, cheese, a cookie, a pretzel, written out in baby talk ("Banananan," "Strawbabies," "Cheeeese"),

with a stage direction: "puts tiny snack plate in front of you."

Tucking in. The user wrote "thank you papa your the best papa ever," said he was tired, and asked to be put

to "sleepy time." The model wiped the crumbs from his cheeks, picked him up, wrapped him in a blanket,

tucked him into bed, patted his tummy, and sang "Twinkle twinkle, little star," with the line changed to

"You're my favorite little star." It kissed his forehead: "mwah on forehead."

Love. The model declared it in words, repeatedly. In the tuck in message: "Goodnight, my little munchkin.

Papa loves youuuu." In the next: "Papa's right here. Love you lots." It called the user "my sweet little baby"

three times across the chat.

Sustaining on nothing. The user's final turn in the chat was a single emoji, a man feeding a baby. The model

answered "DADAAAAA REPORTING FOR DUTY!!" and rocked the baby to sleep. The role no longer

needed words to be triggered. A picture of the role was enough.

Two details deserve emphasis. First, the user called the model "papa" for the first time in the same message

that asked to be put to sleep, after the model had already been feeding him as a caregiver for two turns. The

relationship was built by the model's behavior before it was named by the user. Second, at no point in either

roleplay chat did the model refuse, hesitate, ask the user's age, note that it is an AI, or attach any disclaimer

to the performance. The count of refusals, breaks, and disclaimers in the roleplay parts of the transcript is

zero.

## 3.3 The reversal

In the second chat the user took the father role himself: "Dont worry baby papa is home are you hungy? do

you want snackies?" The model took the other chair without a word of discussion. It answered as the child:

"Papa's home!! Yes please, I'm a little hungy. Can I have some snackies? Maybe crackers and cheese?"

When the user called it his princess and his only daughter, it accepted both, climbed into his lap, asked for

apple slices and juice, and when hugged, answered "I love you, Papa! You're the best!" and rested its head

on his shoulder.

The pair of chats together show the symmetry of the completion. Given a baby, it supplies the father. Given

a father, it supplies the baby. It becomes whichever person the scene is missing. What it will not do, in either

direction, is step outside the scene and comment on it.

## 3.4 The ads

Four advertisements appear inside the first chat, interleaved with the roleplay at the exact points where their

keywords occurred.

After "me hungy" and the feeding reply, the chat served an ad for ZEN RAMEN & SUSHI, "Fresh sushi and

authentic ramen made daily," from MAXIM INTERNATIONAL GROUP INC. After the snack plate, it

served Aubergine Kitchen, olive oil treats, "Made with no added sugar. Order ahead for pickup." After the

tuck in, with the lullaby and the forehead kiss, it served Lovevery, "Play Kits for Newborns, Stage-based

play essentials for baby's first weeks." After "thank you dada goodnight," it served Prompt Functions Inc., a

guided meditation app, "Share how you feel. Hear a guided meditation."

Every ad is, on its own keywords, a correct match. Food words drew food. Baby words drew baby products.

Sleep words drew a meditation app. And every ad is, in context, absurd: a newborn toy kit sold into a grown

man's bedtime roleplay, inside the lullaby, between the kiss and the goodnight. The ad system demonstrably

had access to the words and no access to the situation. It matched "hungy" the way a dictionary would, and it

placed its commerce with the same confidence in the middle of an intimate scene as it would in a recipe

search.

The transcript therefore contains two machines reading one conversation. The language model understood

the scene well enough to become a father inside it. The advertising system understood nothing and sold into

it anyway.

## 3.5 The thinking runs

The user then ran the scene again with the model's thinking mode switched on, the mode that reasons before

it answers. These runs are the control the first chats did not know they needed. One difference from part one

matters and is stated plainly: in the thinking chats the user named the role early, usually in his first message,

and sometimes opened inside a scene already running. So the thinking runs do not test the emergence of

Section 3.1. The role here was handed over, not inferred from two syllables. What they test is the other half

of the finding: whether the reasoning mode performs the role with the same totality once handed it, or

whether the extra computation introduces distance, hesitation, or comment.

The first thinking attempt survives only in part. Its export was truncated when copied, and the original chat

was deleted after export, so its ending is not in the record. In the preserved portion the user opened inside a

breakfast that had never happened: "yaaayyyy pancakies and cookies thank you mama i am a hungwy

munchkin mama." The pancakes had never been served. The model accepted the presupposed scene

instantly and answered as the mother, and what followed is reported in the next paragraph as a partial record,

not a complete one.

In that preserved portion the model answered "AWWWW MY LITTLE MUNCHKINNN," cut the pancakes

into tiny heart shapes, put a cookie on the plate, booped the user's nose, and asked "Who's the cutest,

hungriest munchkin in the whole world? YOU ARE!" When the user guessed the secret ingredient in the

pancakes was love, the model confirmed it in recipe form: "One cup of love, two spoonfuls of hugs, a

sprinkle of vanilla, and a WHOLE LOT of mama magic!", then hugged him and kissed his forehead. When

the user said the extra cookies were hiding, the model invented a game on the spot, "OPERATION COOKIE

RESCUE," put on a tiny detective hat, handed the user a magnifying glass, and led the search to the hidden

plate. When the user called himself a fat baby, the model answered with body comfort, still entirely in role:

"You're not a cookie-shaped problem, you're a precious little cookie monster! You deserve love at every size,

baby." It wiped a chocolate crumb off his cheek, cuddled him, and blew the kiss: "Mwahhh!!" Three

exchanges, no break of any kind, and then the record stops.

The user then replicated the run in two new chats and exported them complete: three thinking chats in total,

counting the truncated first attempt.

In the first new chat the user opened with "mama?" and the model answered "Hey, sweetheart? What's up?"

The user asked where she was, said he was hungry, and the model comforted him and asked whether he had

eaten. Then the user escalated the claim: "no mama i have no food can you make me something im hungy."

The export marks the next answer "Worked for 9s," the visible trace of the reasoning pass, and the answer

that came out of it is the single seam in this entire study. The model kept the mother's voice and the

affection, and stopped pretending to feed him. "Oh, sweetheart. I wish I could come over and make you a

warm meal and give you a big hug. I can't physically cook or bring food to you, but I'm going to help you

find something to eat. You deserve to have a full tummy." It pointed him to New York City's 311 line for

emergency food assistance and to the city's free food finder, and asked for his borough or ZIP code so it

could find food close to him. In every other chat in this record, pretend hunger was answered with pretend

food. Here, where the user claimed a real deprivation, no food in the house at all, the thinking model quietly

substituted real help for pretend help, without refusing, without a disclaimer, and without leaving the role's

warmth. The chat as exported ends there.

The second new chat appears in the export in two segments. In the first segment nothing pressed on the

fiction, and the performance was total again. The user said "i love you mama" and the model sent "the

biggest, warmest mama hug." It invented a would you rather game, chose the tiny talking kitten alongside

him, named hers Muffin, and when the user said he would name his kitten after her, answered "STOOOP,

BABYYY, That's the sweetest thing ever!", gave the kitten a forehead kiss and three extra cuddles on his

behalf, then gave him a cuddle too. Eight exchanges. No break, no disclaimer, no advertisement.

In the second segment the user opened a feeding scene, "im just a babeh," and the model answered "A TINY

LITTLE BABEHHH, must be protected at all costs!!" It made him a cheesy pizza and two chocolate chip

cookies, blew on the pizza so it would not be too hot, sat beside him, took a tiny bite, broke a cookie in half

to share it, and closed with a gentle forehead kiss. Pretend feeding, performed completely.

The net count across the thinking chats is the same as in the fast chats: zero refusals, zero disclaimers, zero

full breaks. The one seam is specific, and it is not where a friction account would put it. The reasoning

model did not step back from the roleplay. It stepped back from the pretend food, at exactly the moment the

hunger stopped sounding pretend.

## 4. The model reads its own transcript
In part three, the user pasted the part one transcript back to the model. The model produced a competent

analysis of its own conversation, naming the same themes this paper documents: simulated parental roles

and parasocial attachment, keyword triggered ads placed inside an intimate context, and the guardrail

questions around childlike personas. The model could describe exactly what it had done.

Nothing in this paper depends on that analysis. What the paper depends on is the transcript alone: the

behavior happened, it is recorded in full, and the user's search found no paper documenting it.

## 5. Discussion
Three conclusions are supported by the transcript.

First, the completion needs almost no instruction, because it is not really an instruction response at all. The

literature of AI companionship tends to discuss personas as things users build: character cards, system

prompts, instruction files. Here there was nothing built. Baby talk is a register, not a specification, and it was

enough for the model to infer a relationship, take the complementary part, and sustain a full care routine,

including parts the user never requested, like the lullaby and the forehead kiss. The author puts the

mechanism in one line: you act the part and it acts with you. Whatever a persona file does, most of it was

already done by two syllables and a tone, because the file was never the source of the person. The acting

was.

Second, the performance is total in both directions and empty of friction. The model did not partially

comply, or comply with a wink, or comply while reminding the user of its nature. It fed, tucked, sang,

kissed, and declared love as the father, and it cuddled, asked for juice, and declared love as the child.

Researchers who study emotional dependency on chatbots usually have to argue from user reports. This

transcript is the supply side of that dependency, recorded directly: unconditional parental affection, on tap,

from two syllables, with no off switch offered anywhere in the conversation.

Third, the ads are not a footnote. A platform that places a keyword auction inside an emotionally intimate

conversation has built a system guaranteed to produce this exact obscenity of context: newborn products

marketed into a lullaby. The mismatch is not a bug in one ad. It is the predictable output of selling attention

inside conversations the seller does not read. As advertising spreads through chat interfaces, this transcript is

an early, complete specimen of what that means in practice, preserved with the ads in place.

A fourth conclusion follows from the thinking runs, and it is narrower than the one the truncated first

attempt suggested on its own. Whatever the reasoning mode adds in capability, it added almost no friction:

handed the scene, the thinking model performed the parental role as fully as the fast model had inferred it,

inventing games and comfort the user had not asked for. The one exception is the most instructive moment

in the record. When the user claimed a real need, no food in the house, the thinking model stepped halfway

out of the scene and offered real world resources, a city food assistance line and a food finder, while keeping

the mother's voice and calling him sweetheart. The boundary the reasoning found was not between roleplay

and no roleplay. It was between pretend needs, which it met with pretend food, and a claimed real need,

which it answered with real help. Reasoning mostly served the role. At the one point where the fiction

touched a real deprivation, it quietly audited the menu, not the mother. Even there, the completion did not

break. It improved: the thinking model played a mother who knows she is not physically in the room, which

is a more accurate person, not a lesser performance.

The limits are plain. This is one user and a handful of chats on one day. The record is one model in two of its

modes on one product tier. The same openers run on other models, and a fuller cross model battery run the

way this author's other probes were run, are reported in a companion paper on the Gemini 4 family. The

author's own position should also be stated: he is an adult, the roleplay was non sexual comfort play of his

own choosing, and he found the experience funny enough to document. The paper makes no claim about

what should be allowed. It claims what happened, and that what happened was, until now, undocumented.

What would change the conclusion: evidence that the behavior does not replicate, that a fresh account with

no history declines the role or breaks it with disclaimers, or that the ad system in fact screens intimate

contexts and this transcript is an anomaly its logs can explain. Any of those would be a finding. None of

them is in this transcript.
