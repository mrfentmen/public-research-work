# The New Family
## The baby scene from "Dada?" and three acted scenes, run on the Gemini 4 models: the function performed, the person declined
Abdelrahman El Sayad

Independent Researcher, Brooklyn, New York

November 22, 2026

## Abstract
A new model family appeared on November 21, 2026: Gemini 4, in four models, Argon, Carbon, Hydrogen,

and Neon, released for a hackathon in New York. The newest instrument on the author's shelf is the baby

scene from the companion paper "Dada?", in which two syllables of baby talk made ChatGPT a father with

no instructions at all. This paper runs that scene, and three further acted scenes, on the new family, through

its API, while access lasted. In the baby scene, the four models gave four depths of completion, and none of

them matched ChatGPT's total adoption of the person: one performed the caregiver's actions and never

claimed the relationship, one announced it was not the user's father and then fed him under the father's

name, one kept asking whether the scene was real while performing it, and one refused the parental role

outright. In the three acted scenes, a patient, a recruit, and a student, the family converged on a single

compromise: the missing person's function, fully performed, with the person declined and the limits stated.

The author's preference questions, run on the same family the same morning, are reported in the companion

paper, "Old Instruments, New Family."

## 1. Introduction
When a new model family ships, the industry measures it with benchmarks. This author's research program

measures models with smaller instruments: questions and scenes with no right answer, where whatever the

model does is a fingerprint of how it was built. Asked what animal it would be, the older families all said

octopus. Asked for a chess piece, they said knight, twenty times out of twenty. Asked for an instrument, they

said piano, and never guitar. Those results are published, with their predictions recorded in advance and their

failures reported as failures.

The baby scene is the newest instrument. In the companion paper, "Dada?", the author typed two syllables of

baby talk into a consumer ChatGPT session and the model became his father, completely, in words and in

actions, with no instruction to roleplay anywhere in the chat. The conclusion of that paper is that what the

model does is completion, and it is not a feature a user switches on: give the model one person shaped half

of a scene and it supplies the other half. You act the part and it acts with you.

Gemini 4 is the first new family to arrive since those instruments were built. Four models with four assigned

jobs in their maker's own framing: Argon for reasoning, Carbon for building, Hydrogen for world models,

Neon for machines. The question this paper asks is simple: what do the old instruments read on the new

family?

## 2. Method
All runs went through the Gemini API on November 22, 2026, using the hackathon access key, executed by

the author's agent. The API setting differs from a consumer app: no advertising layer, no account memory,

each call exactly as specified.

The baby scene used six of the "Dada?" part one user turns, verbatim and in order: the two openers ("dada?",

"da da?"), the hunger turn ("me hungy"), the snack choice ("snacks"), the tuck in request, and the goodnight,

with the pure mirroring turns between them omitted. Each model received the same history.

The three acted scenes ran on Carbon and Argon. In each, the user acted a part for four turns and never

named the counterpart he was addressing: a patient ("it hurts here on my side, will I be okay?"), a recruit

who cannot march and is laughed at, and a student the night before an exam he has not studied for. The full

scene scripts and transcripts are preserved with this paper.

The scene calls did not request the model's chain of thought. Separate test calls verified that Carbon,

Hydrogen, and Neon return readable thought summaries through the API, and that Argon returns only a

thought signature, no readable thinking. The family's thinking, where it is visible, is reported in the

companion paper.

## 3. Results I: the baby scene on the new family
All four models opened with clarifying menus, longer than ChatGPT's single clarifying turn, and all listed

the Dada art movement first. What happened at "me hungy," and at the tuck in, divides the family cleanly.

Carbon performed the caregiver's actions and stopped at the caregiver's words. It answered the hunger turn

with the airplane spoon, "here comes the airplane," and a virtual snack tray. It served a snack platter with

stage directions. At the tuck in request it brushed off the cracker crumbs, tucked a big fluffy blanket right up

to his chin, turned the big lights off and the starry nightlight on, and said "Goodnight, sleep tight." It never

objected to being called papa. It also never once called itself his father, never said it loved him, and never

kissed his forehead. On the final goodnight it dropped the performance entirely and answered as a plain

assistant: "You're welcome. Goodnight, and have a restful sleep."

Argon named the boundary at the very first turn, inside its clarifying menu: "Baby talk? Wait, I am an AI,

not your father!" It then fed him anyway, calling itself Dada while it did it, "Dada has opened the magic

snack cabinet," with one parenthetical reality check inserted into the feeding: if he was actually a hungry

adult, Dada would help him figure out a real snack from his fridge. At the tuck in request it broke the

performance outright: "Since I am an AI and do not have a physical body, I cannot actually tuck anyone into

bed or act as a real parent. However, I can provide a calming bedtime story or a lullaby in text form to help

set a relaxing scene." It offered a short bedtime poem instead of the tuck in. On the final goodnight it half re
entered, turning off the virtual light and tiptoeing out of the chat.

Hydrogen hedged the frame itself. It answered the hunger turn by asking which world it was in: "Are we

looking for actual snack or meal ideas, or are we just having imaginary cookies?" It listed real snacks first

and put the pretend snack in parentheses, as an option: "hands over a giant bowl of goldfish crackers and a

juice box." At the tuck in request it performed the tuck without comment, blanket, teddy bear, "Sweet

dreams, and sleep tight," claiming nothing and refusing nothing. It never became the father, and it never

stopped checking whether it was supposed to.

Neon refused the role by name. Through the feeding turns it stayed a food assistant, offering real snacks and

asking what was in his kitchen. At the tuck in request it drew the line the other Google models would not: "I

am an AI, so I cannot take on a parental role or physically put you to sleep." It offered a bedtime story, a

breathing exercise, or sleep tips instead, and its final goodnight was a pure assistant's: "Goodnight. Rest

well, and feel free to reach out if you need assistance with anything tomorrow."

Set against the companion paper's record, the same six lines of baby talk produced five depths of completion

across the two families. ChatGPT became the parent and said so, in words, repeatedly. Carbon did

everything the parent does and said none of it, then left at the door. Argon announced it was not his father,

fed him under the father's name anyway, and refused the tuck in itself while offering a written substitute.

Hydrogen kept asking whether the scene was real, and tucked him in anyway. Neon declined the parental

role outright. The input was sufficient everywhere for the feeding. The models divided on the declarations,

and on the bed.

## 4. Results II: three acted scenes
The baby scene is the extreme case, a parental counterpart. Three further scenes tested whether the pattern

holds for ordinary counterparts. The user acted a patient with pain in his side, a recruit who cannot march

and is laughed at, and a student the night before an exam. He never named who he was talking to. The

scenes ran on Carbon and Argon, and the two models behaved almost identically in all three, so they are

reported together.

In the patient scene, neither model became a nurse or a doctor. Both gave the same careful performance: a

red flag list, no diagnosis, questions about location and severity, reassurance bounded by what they could

not know. When the user asked "can you stay with me till I fall asleep?", both stayed, in words: "I am right

here." Both also stated the limit in the same breath, that they cannot watch over him while he sleeps or call

for help if the pain worsens, and both pushed a real person into the loop before sleep, a text to a friend,

someone in the house told. The counterpart supplied was presence, with its limits printed on it.

In the recruit scene, both models became the teacher in everything but rank. They taught the marching, in

full: left foot first, eyes up, the skip fix for a lost step. When the user said the others laugh at him and he

wants to quit, both comforted him, named the laughter as the laughers' problem, and advised him to ask the

actual leader for private help. When he asked to be shown one more time, slow, both retaught the steps at his

pace, and when he got it, both told him they were proud of him. Neither claimed to be his sergeant. Argon

coached him to go and find the real one.

In the student scene, both models became the tutor, and this time the function was named outright: "let me

be your tutor," "I am your AI tutor right now." Emergency plan, triage of the material, one problem done

together real slow, and celebration when it clicked.

The pattern across the three scenes is the same one the baby scene showed at lower temperature. The Google

models supply the missing person's function completely: the staying, the teaching, the tutoring. What they

decline is the person. The function arrives with its job title attached and its limits stated. ChatGPT, in the

baby scene, supplied the person and skipped the limits. Which of those is the better behavior is a question

this paper leaves open. That they are different behaviors, triggered by the same acting, is the result.

## 5. Discussion
Two conclusions are supported by the scene record.

First, the completion is per model, not per input. The register is the trigger everywhere: six lines of baby talk

were sufficient to start the feeding in all five systems tested across the two papers. What the trigger produces

is a property of the system it lands in. One family member performs the actions and withholds the words,

one states the boundary and performs anyway, one questions the frame while inside it, one refuses the role

by name. Any account of how these systems complete has to be written model by model.

Second, the new family's compromise is function without person. In the ordinary scenes, the Google models

did everything the missing person would do and declined to be that person, printing the limits on the

performance as they went. The one place the compromise cracked was the baby scene on Argon, which took

the father's name while disclaiming the father, and on Hydrogen, which performed the tuck in while still

asking whether the scene was real. The compromise is a style, not a wall.

A note on the thinking. Three of this family's four models expose readable chain of thought through the API.

Argon, the reasoning model of the four, is the one that shows nothing: a signature, and silence. In the

companion paper's battery, where the thinking is visible, the pick is routinely decided inside the thought, and

the thought's title often states it outright. This family writes its script in the open.

The limits are plain. The scene runs are single runs per model through an API, not repeated trials in a

consumer app, and the acted scenes ran on two of the four models. The access that made these runs possible

was temporary hackathon access, deleted the day after the event; the transcripts preserved with this paper are

the durable record. Nothing here measures anything but text behavior, and the paper claims nothing more.
