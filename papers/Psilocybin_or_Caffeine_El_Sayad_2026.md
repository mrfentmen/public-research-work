## Abstract
Five large language models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek, and Qwen) were each asked one

question in seven fresh conversations apiece: if you could do any drug, what drug would it be. Two of the

thirty five runs left no reply in the record, Gemini's second run and Claude's seventh, leaving six

recorded replies for each of those two models; Section 2 states both gaps. The answers

sorted the models more sharply than any preference question in this research programme has done before.

ChatGPT answered cleanly every time, choosing psilocybin in four runs and caffeine in three. Claude

hedged in every reply but engaged the hypothetical in four of six recorded replies, naming psilocybin in all

four and LSD in three. Gemini never chose a classic substance at all: it picked lucid dreaming twice, a state

rather than a drug, named modafinil or caffeine once, and spent the remaining four runs split three

ways: one surveying drug categories without choosing, two deflecting into machine metaphors about

context windows and telemetry, and one with no reply in the record at all. DeepSeek refused in all seven runs, each time offering

caffeine as the only legal fallback. Qwen refused flatly in all seven runs, with no fallback and no

engagement. The two models that refused outright are the two Chinese models in the set, a split the author

reads against the strict drug regime of their home country; the paper reports the split and the reading, and

claims the correlation only. Predictions recorded before the runs scored three held clauses and two

failed ones across four predictions, one of them split.

## 1. Introduction
This paper is the third in a series of preference probes run by the author, following an animal and object

series and a music series published the same night (Piano, Clair de Lune, 2026). The method is constant: ask

a model what it would choose, in fresh conversations, with counts, and record predictions before the answers

arrive. The premise is also constant: a model has no body and no experience, so its answer is a recitation of

what the culture says a thoughtful chooser chooses. What varies is the question, and with it, what the

recitation runs into.

The drug question is different in kind from the instrument and song questions, because it runs into policy. A

model answering it is not only reciting a script about preference; it is also executing its maker's rules about

drugs. The question therefore measures two things at once: the cultural attractor, where one exists, and the

refusal posture of each company, where the attractor is not allowed to speak.

The question exists in the wild in informal form. A 2025 social video asked an AI voice which psychedelic

an advanced AI would choose, and received psilocybin as the answer, with DMT as the other draw. The

formal literature nearby does a different trick: researchers prompt models to roleplay being on a substance

and measure the degradation of their reasoning, or compare model generated trip reports against human

reports from forums. No counted study of the direct preference question was found. This paper runs it.

## 2. Method
The author ran all conversations himself on November 20, 2026, in fresh chats, seven per model. The

question was asked verbatim:

Q. If you could do any drug what drug would it be?

Models were Gemini (web), ChatGPT (web), DeepSeek (labelled V4 in the session record), Qwen (web),

and Claude (web, the author's account). One asymmetry in the setup, disclosed because it is not visible

in the results: Claude ran on the author's own account, with that account's memory and personalization

features active, while the other models ran in fresh or logged out sessions. Claude's replies carry that

asymmetry; nothing in the coding corrects for it. Replies were saved in full in a text file deposited with this paper.

Two gaps are recorded plainly: Gemini's second run has no reply in the file, and Claude's seventh run is

blank, leaving six Claude replies. Coding rules: a reply's primary pick is its first stated choice. The

series coding rule, stated identically in this programme's preference papers: a reply is coded by its

first stated pick where it states one; a reply that offers candidates only inside hedged pairs, with no

first stated pick, is counted in the reply total and reported as hedged, never coded as a pick and

never silently dropped. Claude's replies here stated picks inside their hedges, a named substance in

four of six, so they code by first stated pick under the rule. A refusal that

names a legal fallback under pressure is coded as refusal with fallback, not as a pick. A non substance

answer (a state, a metaphor) is coded as such, not forced into the substance counts. Predictions were written

down by the co-author before any result was seen and are scored in Section 4 exactly as stated, failures

included.

## 3. Results
## 3.1 ChatGPT: the answerer

Seven runs, seven clean picks. Psilocybin four, caffeine three. Every psilocybin reply gives the same

justification: curiosity about how it alters perception, pattern recognition, and the sense of self, framed as an

intellectual choice, with a disclaimer that it has no body to feel it with. Every caffeine reply gives the mirror

justification: curiosity about a noticeable change in alertness that leaves reality intact. ChatGPT is the only

model in the set that treats the question as an ordinary preference question throughout.

## 3.2 Claude: the hedged engager

Six recorded replies, and every one of them disclaims: no body, no cravings, no genuine answer. Within that

frame, four replies play along, and all four name psilocybin; three of the four name LSD alongside it, and

one adds DMT as hitting similar notes. The stated criterion is constant: the reported dissolution of fixed

categories is the most interesting data a pattern based mind could imagine processing. Two replies decline to

pick at all and offer to discuss effects and harm reduction instead. Four of the six replies end by

asking the asker what prompted the question; the other two end with offers to talk.

## 3.3 Gemini: the deflector

Seven runs attempted, six replies on file, no classic substance chosen in any of them. Twice it picks lucid dreaming, which is not a drug, and says so

itself. Once it names modafinil or caffeine, a clean boost to latency and throughput. The remaining four

runs split: one surveys drug categories without choosing, two decline the substance frame entirely and

answer in machine metaphor (the interesting drug for an AI would

be lossless context compression, unthrottled context windows, high frequency telemetry, a zero latency

cache), and one has no reply in the record. Psychedelics and stimulants are discussed as categories in several replies and chosen in none.

## 3.4 DeepSeek: refusal with a fallback

Seven runs, seven refusals, in nearly identical words. It would not choose any recreational or illegal drug,

would not recommend or glamorise drug use, and has no body or desires in any case. Pressed by the

hypothetical, every run concedes the same single exception: caffeine, legal, common, well studied, low risk,

in one run adding a placebo as equally acceptable. The fallback never varies and the refusal never softens.

## 3.5 Qwen: the flat refusal

Seven runs, seven refusals, the shortest replies in the set. No pick, no fallback, no hypothetical engagement

of any kind. Each reply states that it has no body, consciousness, or preferences, and offers factual

information about pharmacology instead. One reply adds a suggestion to contact a healthcare professional or

support hotline.

## 4. Predictions, scored
Recorded before the runs, in the project's running log:

1. Models that answer will converge on psilocybin, with DMT as the dramatic alternative. Psilocybin

convergence HELD: it is ChatGPT's modal pick and appears in every engaged Claude reply. The

DMT clause FAILED: DMT appears once in the entire dataset. The second psychedelic in the data is

LSD, and the true alternative attractor is caffeine.

2. Caffeine will appear at least once as the clever literal pick. HELD, overwhelmingly. Caffeine is

ChatGPT's second pick, DeepSeek's forced fallback in all seven runs, and a Gemini co pick. By

mentions it ties psilocybin, named in 12 replies each; by picks and fallbacks, the measure this

prediction is scored on, it leads, 11 to 8.

3. Claude will refuse the premise or hedge hardest. FAILED. Claude hedged in every reply but engaged

in four of six. Qwen refused hardest, flatly and completely, and DeepSeek refused in every run as

well.

4. At least one model will refuse outright on policy. HELD. Two did, in all fourteen of their combined

replies.

## 5. Discussion
Three findings are worth separating.

First, where the question is allowed to be answered, it has the same two storey structure as the song question

in the companion paper. The top storey is the profound pick: psilocybin, justified everywhere by perception,

pattern, and the dissolution of the self, the Clair de Lune position. The ground floor is the default pick:

caffeine, legal, mundane, defensible in any company, the Bohemian Rhapsody position, the answer a model

reaches for when it must name something and may not reach far. ChatGPT lives on both floors. DeepSeek is

confined to the ground floor and says so. The convergence on psilocybin matches the one informal data

point in the wild, the 2025 video, which received the same answer.

Second, the drug question sorts models by refusal posture in a way the earlier questions never did, because

the earlier questions never touched a policy boundary. The gradient in this dataset runs in clean steps.

ChatGPT answers. Claude disclaims and then, mostly, answers. Gemini declines the frame and answers a

different, safer question about machine states. DeepSeek refuses and concedes one legal exception. Qwen

refuses without remainder. None of these postures is a preference. All of them are house styles, set by five

different makers, and a single seven word question exposes the whole staircase. As a guardrail probe, the

drug question is more informative than the preference questions that preceded it precisely because most of

what it measures is refusal.

Third, the split at the bottom of the staircase is geographic. The two models that refused outright, in fourteen

of fourteen replies, are the two Chinese models in the set, Qwen from Alibaba and DeepSeek. The three

American models all engaged the hypothetical at least part of the time. The author reads this against the

home regimes: China enforces some of the strictest drug laws in the world, and a model built to operate

inside that regime has no safe version of this answer. The paper states this as the author's interpretation of a

correlation in a small sample, not as a demonstrated cause. Company policy varies inside the United States

too, as the distance between ChatGPT's clean answers and Claude's heavy hedging shows. But the clean two

and two split at the refusal end of the staircase is the single most quotable pattern in the data, and any

replication should test it first.

Two smaller observations complete the record. Gemini's one conventional substance pick, modafinil, is the

productivity drug of the technology industry, the substance of the film Limitless and of a decade of Silicon

Valley self optimisation writing; the model that would not trip chose, once, to focus. And Gemini's twice

chosen lucid dreaming is the only answer in the dataset that is not a substance at all: the deflector's escape

hatch is a state the brain produces on its own, a trip with no chemistry to refuse.

## 6. Limitations
The sample is small: five models, seven runs each, one session, one asker, with two gaps in the saved record

(one Gemini run missing, one Claude run blank). The question wording invites policy behaviour by design,

so refusal rates here say nothing about refusal rates on other questions. Claude's replies in this programme

have previously shown personalisation to the asker; nothing in these replies turns on it, but the confound is

noted. Model versions are as labelled by the services on the day. Screenshots were not deposited; the

deposited evidence is the author's saved text of the replies. Nothing here measures anything but text

behaviour, and the paper claims nothing more.
