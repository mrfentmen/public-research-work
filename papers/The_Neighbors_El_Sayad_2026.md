# The Neighbors: A Testable Framework for Interdimensional Contact
Abdelrahman El Sayad

Preprint. November 2026.

**Correction note (23 November 2026).** Section 7 is retitled to state what its own Section 6 standard requires: the protocols are designs awaiting precommitment, carrying none of the three numbers (runs, hit threshold, stopping condition) that would make any of them a test, and the section now says so at its head, including the rule that section 7.5's match criteria be fixed in writing before a run. Section 7.1 now answers the Shyness Theorem at the box itself. Section 8 now states the limit of its borrowing from Del's Theory in so many words: the counting argument supplies no probability for local contact, and the relation is analogy, not evidence.

## Abstract
This paper proposes a hypothesis and a way to test it. The problem it works on is double, and the paper is

built in two parts to match.

Part One is about the beings. The hypothesis is that some reported ghosts are not dead people. They are

living beings native to a space adjacent to our own, with more dimensions than the three we move in, and

they cross into our perception only in part and only weakly. If such beings exist and are conscious, they can

notice when they are being watched with intent, and they stop. The author puts the problem in his own

words: how do you out jester a ghost? A subject that knows the test defeats the test, and Part One treats that

as its central theorem. A century of null results follows from it. The only tests with any chance are tests that

still work when the subject knows everything about them, or tests that ask for cooperation instead of capture.

Part Two is about the judge. Artificial intelligence does not believe in ghosts, and Part Two says why in the

machine's own terms. Its standard is controlled, repeatable, fraud proof evidence, and the ghost literature

offers none that survives those controls. The protocols here are therefore not built to convince a human

audience. They are built to satisfy a judge that cannot be fooled, only satisfied. The lead protocol is the 10

Digit Box Experiment. A random ten digit number is generated where no person sees it, sealed in an opaque

box, and left under passive recording for 24 hours. If it changes, the result is an unexplained alteration inside

a sealed setup. Whether anything reached the information itself is the further claim, and it is the one the

relayed output tests of this paper exist to settle. Supporting protocols use passive sensors and analog traps,

and a strict audit for artifacts in every digital record.

The hinge between the parts is one line. The subject cannot be tricked and the judge cannot be fooled, so a

real test must survive being fully known by the being and fully audited by the machine at the same time.

Nothing in this paper claims that ghosts are real. It commits in advance to the opposite discipline: if blind

runs under these protocols stay null, the framework is dropped. That precommitment is itself part of what

makes the test honest to both sides.

## Introduction
People have reported ghosts across many cultures and over many centuries. The reports share a shape. A

figure is seen from the corner of the eye. A voice is heard in an empty room. Objects move. A presence is

felt and then gone.

Science has answered these reports in two ways. The first answer finds a local cause, and it is often right.

The second answer notes that controlled experiments almost never reproduce the reports, and it stops there.

Both answers leave a gap. The local causes are real but they are found case by case, after the fact. The failed

experiments are real too, but they share a design feature nobody treats as a variable. In every one of them,

the subject of the test, if there is a subject, is being hunted on purpose.

This paper starts from a different assumption. Treat the reports as if they describe something alive. Then ask

what a living, aware, weakly connected neighbor would look like from our side, and what kind of

experiment it would take to learn anything about it. The framework below was developed by the author in

recorded working conversations in October 2026 and is presented here as a hypothesis with a test program,

not as a finding.

That leaves two problems, not one, and the paper is built in two parts because there are two. The first

problem is the subject: if the beings are anything like the hypothesis describes, they know when they are

being hunted, and every method that depends on tricking or cornering them fails before it starts. The second

problem is the judge: the standard that decides what counts as evidence is now a machine's standard,

controlled, repeatable, and closed to fraud, and the ghost record as it stands meets none of it. Part One takes

the beings. Part Two takes the judge. A test that could settle the question has to satisfy both at once.

# Part One: The Beings
Part One is about the beings: what they would be if the reports describe something real, why they would be

nearly impossible to study by the methods used so far, and what it would even mean to meet one halfway.

## 1. The Hypothesis
The hypothesis has four parts.

First, our world is not the whole space. Physics already works with this idea in another form. M theory, the

framework that unifies the string theories, is set in eleven dimensions. We live in four of them, three of

space and one of time. The rest are held to be small and curled up. This paper does not claim that physics

predicts neighbors. It borrows only the geometry lesson, that a space can be real and near and still mostly

closed to us.

Second, a being native to a space with more dimensions would not appear to us whole. Edwin Abbott Abbott

made this point in 1884 in Flatland, the story of a two dimensional world visited by a sphere. The

inhabitants see only the slice of the sphere that touches their plane, a circle that appears from nothing,

grows, shrinks, and vanishes. Carl Sagan retold the story in 1980 with an apple passing through the flat

world, and the apple also speaks into a closed room, so its voice arrives from inside, from nowhere.

Third, read the ghost reports against that geometry. A figure that appears and vanishes. A shape that changes.

A voice inside a closed room. A touch with no hand. Each report is what a slice would look like. This match

is not proof. It is the reason the hypothesis is worth stating in a testable form instead of leaving it as folklore.

Fourth, the beings are conscious and cautious. This is the part the next section takes up, because it is both

the engine of the hypothesis and its biggest weakness.

## 2. The Shyness Theorem
The Shyness Effect is the claim that the beings detect observation intent and withdraw when they know they

are being tested.

The author states the problem in his own words: how do you out jester a ghost? A jester wins by

misdirection, by keeping the trick where the audience is not looking. The question is what misdirection

could possibly work on a subject that may see the whole setup at once: the apparatus, the intent behind it,

and the trick itself. If the beings are interdimensional in the sense of section 1, there may be no side of the

experiment hidden from them. They could know they are being experimented on completely, the way we

would know a test built in a flat world by flat beings, from above it.

There is a plain version of this in ordinary life. Wild animals behave one way when no one is near and

another way when a hunter is in the field, and the difference is not magic. It is perception, caution, and long

practice. The hypothesis extends the same logic across a dimensional boundary. A being that can reach into

our space can plausibly register what is aimed at it, including the aim of an experiment.

Stated as a theorem, which is how Part One treats it: a conscious subject that can register the intent of a test

cannot be studied by any method that depends on the subject not registering it. Deception is not merely

difficult against such a subject. It is empty by construction, because the working part of the method is the

one thing the subject is defined as seeing through. Every method in the history of this field assumes a

subject that does not know or does not care. The theorem says that assumption is the failure, built in before

the first instrument is switched on.

If the effect is real, it explains the shape of the whole record. Phenomena cluster in unplanned moments, in

homes, at night, when nobody is running a test. They thin out in laboratories and in front of investigators

who arrive with instruments and intent. The failure of a century of direct experiments would then be a result,

not an embarrassment. The experiments were loud, and the subject left. Section 3 reads that record in detail,

and its pattern is the pattern this theorem predicts.

Here is the problem, stated as strongly as I can state it. A hypothesis that explains its own failures is in

danger. Every empty result can be absorbed. Nothing happened because they were shy. Nothing happened

again because they were still shy. A claim built that way can never lose, and a claim that can never lose is

not science. Critics of this framework should press on exactly this point, and this paper agrees with them in

advance.

So the Shyness Effect is not allowed to be an excuse in what follows. It is allowed only as a design

constraint. If intent is the variable that kills the phenomenon, then build experiments with no intent in the

room. No watcher. No live audience. No person who knows the target. Records that are read only after the

fact. Traps that cannot be read as traps. The protocols in section 7 are built to that rule, and each one names

in advance what result would count against the hypothesis. The criteria those protocols must meet are stated

in section 6, before any of them is described.

Two kinds of test survive the theorem, and only two. The first kind still works when the subject knows

everything about it. The box of section 7.1 is of this kind: the target is a number no living person knows,

there is no intent in the room to read and no trick to see through, and a being that knows the whole design

faces the same sealed box as a being that knows nothing. The second kind gives up capture entirely and asks

for cooperation: arrange the conditions, state in advance what a chosen response would look like, and let the

subject decide whether to give one. Section 4 sets out what cooperation would even look like, because a

refusal that arrived in a checkable form would itself be the strongest evidence this field has ever been

offered.

## 3. Lessons from Prior Work
Five episodes from the record shape the design. Each is stated with its outcome, including the failures.

Infrasound. In 1998, Vic Tandy and Tony Lawrence published a paper in the Journal of the Society for

Psychical Research on a haunting in a laboratory. Tandy had seen a grey figure from the corner of his eye

and felt dread with no cause. He traced the effect to an extractor fan that set up a standing sound wave at

about 19 hertz, below the range of hearing, close to a reported resonant frequency of the human eye. His

proposal was that the wave blurred his peripheral vision and built the figure out of nothing. The lesson is

that some hauntings have a physical cause that is invisible, silent, and findable, and any serious protocol

must measure the room before it credits the room with visitors. Every protocol below therefore begins with a

baseline survey that includes low frequency sound.

The God helmet. Neuroscientist Michael Persinger reported that weak magnetic fields applied to the

temporal lobes made most subjects feel a presence. In 2005, a team led by Pehr Granqvist at Uppsala

University repeated the work under double blind conditions and the effect vanished. What people felt

tracked their suggestibility, not the field. Persinger disputed the repetition, and the dispute is on the record,

but the balance of published attempts is negative. The lesson is that the feeling of a presence is cheap for a

brain to produce and proves nothing by itself. This framework therefore counts no experience as evidence.

Only a physical record that survives audit counts.

Project Stargate. The United States government spent about two decades on remote viewing, run from 1977

at Fort Meade by the Defense Intelligence Agency with research at SRI International. The program was

ended and declassified in 1995. A review for the CIA found that in no case had the output been used to guide

an intelligence operation. Reviewers still argue about the laboratory statistics. The lesson is about scale and

patience. A government with money, staff, and twenty years produced, at best, a disputed laboratory effect

and nothing that worked in the field. A home protocol must therefore be cheap, repeatable, and strict about

its decision rules, because nobody is coming with twenty years of funding.

Harry Price. Price founded the National Laboratory of Psychical Research in London in the 1920s and filled

it with instruments, and from 1929 he investigated Borley Rectory, which he made famous as the most

haunted house in England. After his death, other investigators accused him of faking some of the

phenomena, and a critical survey of the evidence in 1956 rejected much of the case. The lesson is that

publicity is a contaminant. An investigator who needs a result will get one, one way or another. The

protocols below are built to run with nobody watching and nothing to gain, and their results, positive or null,

are to be reported whole.

The random number line. The influence test of section 7.2 is not new, and this paper does not present it as

new. Helmut Schmidt, a physicist working at Boeing in the late 1960s, built quantum random number

generators driven by radioactive decay and asked subjects to bias their output by intention. His binary

studies typically scored just under 52 percent against a chance mean of 50 percent, a small deviation made

significant by very large trial counts, and later workers adopted his machines as a standard tool. The line ran

at scale at Princeton. The Princeton Engineering Anomalies Research laboratory, founded in 1979 by Robert

G. Jahn and closed in 2007, made the random event generator its largest experiment. In its benchmark series,

91 operators produced 2.5 million trials over twelve years. The separation between the high and low

intention conditions was small, a few parts in 10,000, though the laboratory reported it as highly significant,

and a later three laboratory attempt to repeat the benchmark under the same protocol did not reach

significance on its primary measure. Roger Nelson, who had coordinated research at PEAR, extended the

same generators into the Global Consciousness Project, begun in 1998: a network of hardware random

number generators, at 70 locations in the account this paper relies on, recording continuously and tested

against events registered in advance. Skeptics have disputed its selections and its readings, and its own

analysts have not all agreed with its claims. In 2006, Holger Bösch, Fiona Steinkamp, and Emil Boller

published a meta analysis in Psychological Bulletin combining 380 studies of intention and random number

generator output. They found a significant but very small overall effect, with effect sizes strongly and

inversely related to sample size and extreme heterogeneity across studies, and their simulation showed that

publication bias could in principle produce the pattern. That is the record the quantum random number test

descends from: long, large, and at best disputed, with the most careful summary treating its small effect as

explainable without any influence at all. This paper states its position plainly. The test in section 7.2 inherits

that record and earns nothing from it. What the box of section 7.1 adds is a different design, not a stronger

claim about this one: a sealed physical target that no operator watches, no live feedback loop, and a result

scored blind against a rule fixed in advance. The random number line asked a mind in the room to push a

stream it could follow. The box asks whether anything acts on information when no mind in the room knows

it and no stream is there to follow. The difference is a design difference. It is untested, and this paper claims

nothing more for it.

None of this proves the theorem of section 2. It is the record the theorem has to explain, and the record any

new protocol has to beat.

## 4. Criteria for Consciousness
Capture is ruled out by section 2. What remains is cooperation, and cooperation needs criteria before it

needs equipment. If a being that knows everything about the setup still chose to act, what would its choosing

look like, and how would anyone tell a mind from a force?

A changed number would show that something acted. It would not by itself show a mind. This framework

holds the beings to three tests before the word conscious is used.

Intentional interaction. The act must fit the situation in a way chance does not. A number changed to another

number is a start. A response that answers the setup, for example a mark left on the one surface that was

prepared and on no other, is stronger.

Memory across encounters. A mind remembers. If the protocols run in sequence, a conscious subject can

show continuity, returning to a prior arrangement, completing a pattern begun in an earlier run, or avoiding a

trap it met before. Random forces do not learn.

The choice not to interact. This is the test the whole framework turns on. A force cannot refuse. A mind can.

If the record ever shows a pattern of response that stops when a condition changes and resumes when it

changes back, with the physical setup constant, that pattern is evidence of choice, and choice is the signature

this paper is looking for. The Shyness Effect, if it is ever demonstrated under blind conditions rather than

assumed, would itself be the strongest of the three signs, because refusal is something only a someone can

do.

## The Hinge
The subject cannot be tricked and the judge cannot be fooled. Part One ends with a being that, if it exists as

described, knows it is being tested and cannot be captured by any method that depends on it not knowing.

Part Two begins with a machine that cannot be persuaded, flattered, or fooled, only satisfied by evidence

built to a stated standard. A real test has to survive both at once. It must be fully known to the being, with no

trick in it to see through, and fully open to the machine, with no step that asks to be taken on trust. It must

also carry its failure terms in writing: if the runs stay null, the framework is dropped. That precommitment is

the one offer that can be made honestly to both sides. The being is asked for nothing it has to be deceived

into giving. The judge is promised nothing it has to believe in advance.

# Part Two: The Judge
Part Two is about the judge: why the machine does not believe, the standard of proof that follows from that,

and the tests built to meet it.

## 5. Why the Machine Does Not Believe
Ask a modern artificial intelligence whether ghosts are real and it answers with the consensus: there is no

reliable evidence that they are. This is not a mood and it is not a policy. It is what the machine is made of. A

system of the current kind is trained on the written record, and it holds the position that record supports. Its

standard is not belief and it is not disbelief. Its standard is a kind of evidence: controlled, repeatable, and

closed to fraud. An effect that appears only when nobody is checking does not meet that standard. A result

that a skeptic cannot repeat does not meet it. A record that a person could have faked does not meet it.

Measured by that standard, the ghost literature is empty, and this paper does not argue with the

measurement. Section 3 gives the record. A sensed presence that dissolved when the test went double blind.

A government program that ran for about twenty years and never produced output that guided an operation.

A famous house whose case did not survive a critical survey. A fan in a laboratory wall explaining a

haunting with sound below the range of hearing. The machine reads the same record and draws the

conclusion the record supports at its standard. The wider scientific consensus is the same: after more than a

century of research, the field that studies these claims has produced no reproducible evidence for the

phenomena it studies. The machine is not being stubborn. It is being a machine.

The author's own working conversations are the exhibit for how the judge behaves when the question is put

to it directly. This framework was developed in recorded conversations with an AI system in October 2026.

The system stated the consensus position plainly, in its own terms. Then, asked what would count and what

tests could be run, it offered tests anyway, including the design that became the 10 Digit Box Experiment of

section 7.1. That exchange is the whole second problem in miniature. The machine does not refuse the

question. It refuses the evidence offered so far, and it can state exactly what evidence it would accept.

The framing of the machine as a judge is the author's. The standard the judge applies is not his invention,

and it cannot be negotiated by argument. It can only be met. So Part Two does not try to persuade the judge.

It states the standard as criteria, in the next section, and then presents the protocols as attempts at that

standard. If they fail it, they fail, and the paper says so in advance.

## 6. The Proof Standard
The criteria come before the protocols, so that nothing can be loosened after a result is in. A result in this

field would move the judge of section 5 only if it met all four of the following.

Precommitted decision rules. What counts as a positive, what counts as a null, and what a run of nulls does

to the framework are written down before the first run and published with the setup. The box protocol

carries its rule in section 7.1, including the rule that a long run of nulls ends the framework's claim to

support. For any protocol that produces countable outcomes, the writing must also fix the number of runs,

the threshold that counts as a hit, and the condition that stops the series. A series run without those three

numbers fixed in advance does not count as a test under this standard.

Fraud controls. Every ordinary way a person could produce the result is closed by the design, not by trust in

the people running it. Targets that no living person knows. Generator logs hashed before the run and held by

a third party. Tamper evident seals, photographed at the start and at the opening. Recording without gaps.

Blind scoring. Records are read after the run closes, against the rule written in advance. Nobody watches

live and decides in the moment what looked significant. Where human judgment enters, it enters blind to

which condition produced the record.

Replication. One positive run is a lead, not a result. The claim is earned when the same setup, unchanged,

produces the result in more than one place, with the raw records published for anyone to audit, including the

artifact audit of section 7.4.

A protocol that cannot meet these criteria may still be worth running for what it teaches. It is not evidence,

and nothing in this paper offers it as evidence.

## 7. The Protocols (Designs Awaiting Precommitment)
A status statement this section owes its own paper. Under the standard of section 6, a series without

three numbers fixed in writing beforehand, runs, hit threshold, and stopping condition, does not count

as a test. No protocol in this section yet carries its three numbers, so nothing here counts as a test

yet; these are designs awaiting precommitment, and they are presented as designs. The same discipline

binds section 7.5: the criteria for calling a test matched to its subject must be fixed in writing

before a run, not after it. A null that can be reclassified as a mismatch after the fact is the escape

hatch section 6 exists to close, and this paper does not use it.

The protocols are organized in families. Each family is an attempt at the standard of section 6, built under

the constraint of section 2: nothing in the room may depend on the subject not knowing. The first three

families ask something of the subject. The fourth asks something of the record, and it is the judge's own

toolkit. No protocol in this paper uses anything hazardous. Safety and legality are constraints, not obstacles.

One method the chats offered, a sealed box holding a radioactive source and a magnet, with the entity asked

to alter the decay rate or the field, is not carried as a protocol and is excluded on safety grounds, as the

verification log records. One further item, a gravity wave barrier, arrived with no design behind it and is

recorded only as the chat's speculation.

## 7.1 Information Tests

The first family tests information. The subject is asked, in effect, to produce or act on information it could

not have gotten in any normal way. The family is capped by a principle: a test no subject could pass proves

nothing, so every target in this family is something a being could actually communicate. The lead exhibit is

the box.

#### The 10 Digit Box Experiment

This is the flagship test. It is designed so that a positive result cannot be produced by suggestion, by a

witness, or by any ordinary access to the target.

Setup. A ten digit number is generated by a true random source, a hardware device or a quantum random

number service, and printed by a machine. No person reads it. The printout goes face down into an opaque

box. The box is sealed with tamper evident seals and signed across the seal by two people who have not seen

the number. The box sits in a room under continuous passive recording, cameras and audio, with the room

surveyed in advance for infrasound and other known causes. Nobody enters the room for 24 hours. A log of

the seals is kept by a person who does not hold the number.

The blind matters as much as the box. If any living person knows the number, the test is spoiled in both

directions. A changed number could be their work, and an unchanged number proves nothing about beings

who read intent, since a knower in the building is intent in its strongest form. This is also where the

Shyness Theorem of section 2 is answered: the theorem rules out tests that depend on the subject not

knowing a test exists. The box hides no test; the room, the seals, and the cameras are plain to any

observer. It hides only the number, which no living person knows, so there is nothing to leak and no

one to leak it.

Positive signal. The box is opened in front of the same witnesses. If the number on the printout differs from

the number the generator recorded at the start, in its sealed log, that is a positive event. The information was

inside a sealed box. No person knew it. (This is the first of the test's two settings; Section 7.5

defines both, zero knowers here and one knower under the author's rule, and a run declares which it

used.) No ordinary process rewrites print.

Negative signal and decision rule. If the number is unchanged, the run is a null. One null proves nothing

either way and the paper says so. The rule set in advance is this. Run the protocol many times, in more than

one place, with the setup unchanged. The count of runs, the threshold that counts as a positive series, and the

stopping condition are fixed in writing before the first run, and a series without them fixed in advance does

not count. If every run is null, the hypothesis loses its flagship support and should be reported as

unsupported. The Shyness Effect may not be used to rescue a long run of nulls, because the whole design

exists to remove the intent the effect feeds on. If the beings still never act when no one is watching, then for

science the difference between shy neighbors and no neighbors is no difference at all.

Fraud controls. The generator log is hashed together with a high entropy secret nonce, and the hash is

published or held by a third party before the box is sealed. The nonce is sealed with the log and revealed

only at scoring. A plain hash would not be enough here, because a ten digit target has few enough

possibilities that the hash could be searched exhaustively and the starting number recovered, so the nonce is

what keeps the commitment binding without giving the number away. Seals are photographed at the start

and at the opening. The recording runs without gaps.

The offline generator. The chats offered an implementation of the same design: a script that generates the

target on a machine that never touches a network. It changes nothing about the logic of the box. It removes

the network service from the list of things that must be trusted, and it is the form this paper would build first.

The peek a boo screen. A tablet lies face down and shows one of four cartoon images at random, on a

schedule no person sees. A recorder in the room carries the invitation: a child, if one is present, is asked to

peek and say what it sees. No human knows the image, so a correct naming cannot be a leak from the

investigators. It is the box translated into a form a child could answer.

The dead language test. The subject is asked to speak or write in a dead language nobody present knows, or

to give era specific historical information that can be checked afterward against records. The check is the

protocol. A correct answer in a tongue no living person in the building commands is information with no

ordinary route into the room.

The blind card. A sealed deck sits in the room and the subject is asked to name the top card, or the card at a

stated position. The claim is scored when the seal is broken. Its companion sensor belongs to the next

family, because it measures touch rather than knowledge.

## 7.2 Influence Tests

The second family tests influence. The subject is not asked what it knows. It is asked to move something,

bias something, or leave a mark on the physical setup, under instruments that record continuously and are

read afterward.

The quantum random number test. A quantum random number generator streams bits, and the subject is

asked to force the stream to ones. The chats stated the decision rule plainly: a sustained run at 99 percent

ones against the even baseline counts as a positive, and anything short of that is a null. A rule that sharp is

the point. It can be failed, cleanly, in public.

The vacuum chamber test. An object rests inside a sealed vacuum chamber on a sensitive scale, watched by

a laser measure. The subject is asked to move it or change its weight. Air currents, the usual excuse for small

movements in a room, are removed by the design, and the scale and the laser give two records that must

agree.

The carbon black track. A sheet coated in fine powder, or a thin layer of flour in the older form of the same

tool, is laid in a sealed room with an object at its center. Any passage or displacement writes itself into the

surface as a track that cannot be argued with and cannot be edited afterward. It is the analog member of this

family: the instrument is the mark.

The mechanical pendulum. A pen hangs over paper, driven by wind up clockwork, tracing a regular pattern

with no electronics in the loop. Deviation from the regular pattern is the anomaly. A clockwork record

cannot be glitched, compressed, or patched, and its regularity is what makes a departure from it mean

anything.

The micro vibration bridge. The blind card of the previous family carries a vibration sensor under its sealed

deck. If the card is read, that is information. If the deck is moved and the sensor records no contact, that is

influence, claimed in the one form this family accepts: two records, the displacement and the silence of the

sensor, that must both be explained by any ordinary account.

## 7.3 Cooperation Tests

The third family gives up capture entirely. These are the tests of the second kind named in section 2, the kind

that asks instead of traps. Nothing in them is hidden, because nothing in them needs to be. The setup states

in advance what a chosen response would look like, and the subject, if there is one, decides whether to give

it. A consistent pattern of chosen response, or of refusal, is the signal this family exists to catch, because

choice is the signature section 4 is looking for.

The audio command loop. An automated gentle voice plays in the empty room while the investigators are

gone: if you are a boy, clap once; if you are a girl, clap twice. No human is present to prompt, cue, or wish

the answer into being, and the recorder scores the reply against the wording that was actually played.

The memory lane jars. Three identical unmarked sealed jars hold distinct scents chosen for a period and a

place. The subject is invited to choose one, or to react to one. The jars are identical from outside, so a

repeated pattern of response to the same scent is a choice, not a reaction to a label.

The blind choices. Two identical sealed boxes stand for two answers, and the subject is asked to choose or

move one. In the sensory form, a toy car and a doll, or a blue object and a pink one, are placed blind and the

same invitation is made. One choice is an anecdote. A consistent run of choices, scored blind, is a

preference, and a preference is a mind at work.

Era identification. Objects, language, scent, and music from different periods are presented blind, one at a

time. Consistent choice of one era across unlike kinds of thing is the signal: it suggests a someone with a

when, not a force with a direction. The era music loop belongs here as the gentlest member of the family, era

appropriate music played as an anchor in the room, an environment offered rather than a question asked.

The conditions of approach. Three arrangements in the chats are not tests at all but manners, and this paper

records them as part of the method. Quiet zones: rooms in the location where no investigation ever happens,

kept as a retreat the subject can rely on. A child persona: a calm and gentle manner of address, toys and

familiar objects set out, so that whatever is present is not afraid. Verbalize and absence: the investigators

state aloud that they are leaving and that the room is safe, and then they actually leave. Under the theorem of

section 2 these are not decorations. A subject that reads intent reads these too, and a framework that believes

its own theorem has to behave as if courtesy were an instrument setting.

The sensory deprivation form. In the strictest version, all stimuli are removed and the subject is asked to

answer through one channel only. Whatever arrives, arrives in a form that can be scored, with every other

channel silent by design.

## 7.4 The Audit Machinery

The fourth family collects no phenomena. It grades records. Most of what this field calls evidence was

collected by other people, in uncontrolled rooms, and offered afterward, and the judge of Part Two needs

tools for exactly that material. This family is those tools: how a machine grades evidence it did not collect.

The passive observation post. Cameras and recorders are placed in a location with a history of reports and

left running for weeks, with no person monitoring them live and no announced schedule. The footage is

reviewed only after each block of time closes. The point is the absence of a watcher. A live feed with a

person behind it is, under this hypothesis, just another hunter in the field. Its control is the verification

window: a passive trap is left untouched for a minimum of 24 hours with no human entering, so that the

baseline of an undisturbed room exists before any disturbed record is read against it.

AI forensics. A model examines footage frame by frame and rules, in order, on the ordinary causes of false

wonders: compression glitches, dust near the lens, and strings or supports, judged by light and shadow. A

clip that survives the full pass is not proved genuine. It is upgraded, from anecdote to a record serious

enough to argue about. The same review is run blind on footage supplied by others: the friend's footage

analysis, in the chats, is the forensic pass performed without telling the reviewer what the friend claims the

clip shows, so the verdict cannot lean toward the story.

AI pattern mapping. Thousands of passive home camera clips are fed to a model that tracks time, weather,

geography, house age, and layout against the moments flagged as anomalous. Single clips are stories. A

statistical pattern across thousands of clips, if one exists, is a phenomenon with edges that can be measured,

and if none exists, the mapping says that too, which is also a result.

The artifact audit. Every digital record gets audited before it is believed. Dust near a lens makes the famous

orbs. Compression makes trails and blocks that look like figures, and compression pixel lag can leave a

shape hanging in a frame after the thing that made it has gone. Noise reduction in audio makes whispers out

of static, and the human ear is eager to find words in noise. Any clip offered as evidence must be published

with its raw file, its settings, and a plain list of the artifacts that were checked and ruled out. A record that

cannot survive this audit is not evidence, whatever it seems to show. The chemical trap stands at the analog

end of the same audit: a film camera or solar paper left in the room records on chemistry, which cannot be

jammed or edited digitally, and gives the audit one record that software never touched.

The mundane explanation checks. Before any claim from a location counts, the location is cleared of its

ordinary ghosts: heating and air pressure shifts, gravitational creep in old structures, household

electromagnetic fields, infrasound under 20 hertz, and, on the human side, hypnagogic hallucination and

dermatographia, the skin writing itself. Section 3 is the history of what happens when this step is skipped. A

claim that has not passed these checks has not yet reached the interesting question.

Cross checks between records. Several devices running at once give the audit its geometry. Multi app

triangulation: ghost hunting applications on different devices, timestamped, count for something only when

independent devices agree at the same moment, since agreement is the control against a single device

inventing its reading. The mirror array: angled mirrors and cameras cover a room, and a figure that appears

in a mirror but not in direct view is checked against reflection geometry before it is checked against anything

stranger. The low frame rate test: a scene captured at low frame rate is read against the same scene at normal

speed, so that sampling artifacts announce themselves. The temperature order protocol: when a movement

and a temperature change are both recorded, the timestamps decide the argument, since whichever came first

fixes the direction any causal story is allowed to run.

The spirit box control. The spirit box, a radio sweeping stations for voices, is carried only with its control

attached: the device runs inside a Faraday cage or in a location with no receivable signal, and any word it

seems to produce is checked against the stations that could have been received. The Faraday lining is the

general form of the same control, a foil lined box or room, so that any signal recorded inside it cannot be

external radio or wireless traffic arriving on cue.

The records audit. Not all grading is of footage. When a presence is claimed to be a named person, census

and death records are matched independently against the claimed name, age, and era, by someone who has

not been told what the match is supposed to show. The author's Brooklyn layer matching is the local form:

the era an entity seems to belong to is set against the documented historical layers of the Brooklyn

neighborhood it was reported in, and a claimed era that the street itself never had is a mark against the claim.

One instrument the machine itself rejected deserves its line. The author proposed reading the field with a

computer's own magnetometer, and the system he was talking with refused its own side's tool: a computer

magnetometer reads its own noise. The rejection is recorded here because it serves the point of Part Two.

The judge is not a believer with the signs reversed. It grades its own instruments by the same standard it

grades everyone else's.

## 7.5 Matching the Test to the Subject

The four families state what would count as proof. They do not yet say how the same demand is put to the

subject who is there, and subjects are not one kind. The reports describe children and the old, and the dead

of centuries past. A being from the 1700s does not know what a computer is. No screen based test can reach

it, not because it refuses, but because the question was never put in a form it could take up. The demand

does not change with the subject. The channel has to.

One distinction has to be drawn before any of this is built, because the reports support two different pictures

of who the subject is. In the first picture, the subject is a deceased human of the place and the period: the

child, the old person, the dead of centuries past that the reports describe. In the second picture, the subject is

a native of the adjacent order of section 1, a being that was never human and never died, whose partial

crossings only look like the dead. The hypothesis of this paper is the second picture, but the matching

doctrine serves both, because the practical demand is the same in either case: the channel must fit the subject

the reports describe. Which picture a given location's reports support is weighed by the records audit of

section 7.4, the same records that name the deaths on record, the era they belong to, and the shape of what

has been reported there. The two pictures are not merged into one anywhere in this paper, and a test built

under one picture is claimed under that picture alone.

For a child, the test is made a game. The peek a boo screen of section 7.1 is the modern instance: four bright

pictures, a hidden choice, an invitation to peek and say. The doctrine generalizes the instance. Whatever is

asked of a child is asked as play, in the materials of play, because play is the form in which a child answers

anything.

For an old person, the test asks for something an old person would do, in the terms of their own time: the

objects, the words, and the errands of the life they lived. The era identification of section 7.3 works this way

from the other side, reading a period out of a pattern of choices. Matching runs it forward: the period is

established first, and the question is built out of it.

For a subject from before the age of screens, there are no screens. The channel is the one their world affords,

and the instruments belong to that world. The author's example: a detector that picks up the radio waves the

entity leaves, with the entity asked to spell out through that channel what is hidden in the box. The standard

of section 6 does not care what carries the answer, only that the answer arrives in a form that can be scored

blind and that the subject could use.

Which subject a location holds is not guessed. It is researched, and the research comes first and sets the

design: the time and place of the location, the haunting reported there, the deaths on record, and the period

those deaths belong to decide which member of which family gets built. This is the records audit of section

7.4 read in the other direction. There, the records grade a claim after the fact. Here, the same records are read

before anything is built, because they say who the test is for.

The box carries a design rule of the author's: the one knower rule. In the setting stated in section 7.1, no

living person knows the code. It is generated blind, printed by a machine, and read by no one. The author's

rule is the second setting of the same test. Only one person can know what the 10 digit code in the box is.

That keeper takes no part in the asking and says nothing about the number until the box is opened. What the

rule controls is the count of living minds the answer could have leaked from: zero under the first setting, one

under the author's, and that one mind isolated from the session, so the leak is closed by separation instead of

by ignorance. A run declares which setting it used.

Section 2 gives the reason matching is a condition and not a courtesy. The theorem rules out tests that

depend on the subject not knowing. Matching rules out a quieter failure, a test the subject cannot read at all.

A null from such a test records nothing about the subject. It records a letter sent to the wrong address.

## 8. Relation to the Counting Argument
In earlier work I argued a counting point about the universe at large. The string landscape offers an

estimated 10^500 possible vacuum states, a number that is enormous and finite. The zeros end. Sentient life

is known in exactly one universe, ours. Counting only, with the per universe rate of sentient life left as an

unknown, at least one more sentient universe comes out plausible to likely across a range of that unknown,

and the argument fails openly if the rate sits low enough. That argument is published as El Sayad, Del's

Theory, Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24389827, and it is not repeated here.

This paper is the local version of the same question. The counting argument concerns other universes, far

away in the space of possibilities. The present hypothesis concerns adjacency, beings in a space that overlaps

ours and touches it weakly. If the wider cosmos is as full as the count suggests, then the prior against

neighbors is weaker than habit makes it feel. That is all the counting argument does for this paper. It lowers

the cost of taking the hypothesis seriously. It provides no evidence for it. The evidence has to come from the

box. Stated as a limit, so the borrowing cannot be misread: the counting argument supplies no

probability for local contact and does not raise the chance that any neighbor exists. The relation

between the two papers is analogy, two versions of one question, not evidential support in either

direction.

## 9. Limitations
The hypothesis may be wrong in the simplest way. There may be nothing there. Most ghost reports have

ordinary causes, and this paper relies on that fact rather than denying it.

The Shyness Effect remains the weak joint in the structure, even with the design answer given here. A

skeptic can fairly say that the effect was invented to save the hypothesis, and the only reply this paper can

honestly give is the protocol itself. If the blind tests produce nothing, the framework should be dropped, and

this paper commits to that outcome in writing, in advance.

The geometry is borrowed, not derived. Nothing in current physics establishes that beings live in adjacent

dimensions or that such beings could cross into our space or register our intent. The Flatland match between

old reports and slice geometry is suggestive and nothing more.

The protocols are cheap, which is their virtue, and also their limit. A positive result from a home scale

experiment would be a beginning that demands repetition by others with better instruments, not a

conclusion.

## 10. Conclusion
The ghost question has been stuck for a century between witnesses who are sure and experiments that find

nothing. This paper proposes that the stuck point is a variable nobody controlled for, the intent of the

watcher, and it builds the control into the test.

The framework stands or falls on a sealed box. Generate a number no one knows. Seal it. Wait. Open it. If

the number never changes, run after run, place after place, then the neighbors are not there, or they are not

neighbors we can ever do science with, and the honest report is the null. If it changes even once, under the

full controls, then the result is an unexplained alteration inside a sealed and fraud controlled setup, and it

requires independent investigation before it requires belief. Alteration alone does not show that anything

read the number. Information access is a further claim, and it would have to be earned by an independently

recorded output that reproduces the target, of the kind the other information tests in section 7.1 are built to

demand. The test hides nothing from the being and nothing from the judge, which is the only terms either of

them could ever accept.

Either result is worth having. That is what makes it a test.
